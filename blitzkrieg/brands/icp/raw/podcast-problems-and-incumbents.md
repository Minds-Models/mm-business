# Data, measurement and retail-media technology vendors (retail media ad-tech / in-store analytics / shelf-recognition vendors)

**Self vs other:** Unlike the earlier corpus-wide finding that buyer pain is mostly narrated by vendors, the vendor party itself turned out to be well represented in its own voice: CEOs and senior sales/BD executives from Pentaleap, Zitcha, STRATACACHE/Walkbase, Koddi, and Ailet all spoke in the first person about their own commercial obstacles — market fragmentation eating their addressable budget, having to personally evangelize a still-unproven category before getting a meeting, and the discomfort of walking a budget owner back from an inflated ROAS number to a real one. This is likely because several of these episodes are effectively founder/exec profile interviews built around 'why we exist,' which naturally surfaces the problem-in-their-own-words. The one important gap: on data access/walled retailer data and on pricing commoditization, the corpus only has non-vendor voices (brands, analysts) describing the vendor's world from outside — those two are demoted to runners-up specifically because self-evidence never showed up for them.

## 1. Retail media is structurally fragmented — dozens of walled gardens, each internally fragmented again by channel (on-site, video, email, offsite) — which both erodes the addressable budget that reaches any vendor who isn't Amazon, and makes it genuinely hard for buyers to tell competing vendors apart.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It shrinks the pool of money that ever reaches a non-Amazon vendor (Reiffen: brands are 'probably better off' just optimizing their Amazon spend rather than dealing with fragmentation), and it creates a positioning problem that costs vendors deals and forces defensive partnerships (Pentaleap and Zitcha, nominal competitors, ended up partnering specifically because buyers couldn't tell who did what).

**Self evidence:** Andreas Reiffen (CEO, Pentaleap), ep. 187 & 328: 'today it can be a nightmare because you are approached by different sellers from a multitude of walled gardens and everyone wants some tiny slice of your overall marketing budget... it's so fragmented that you're probably better off to put all your money just to optimize your large chunk of Amazon ad spend.' / 'As there were so many different retail media networks emerging, fragmentation became a problem... even the small walled garden is fragmented.' / 'my perception is it's very very hard to understand who's actually in the space and who does what. And if you ask buyers, sometimes they are very confused.'

**Other-party evidence:** A Henkel eCommerce exec, ep. 320: 'what we lack in the industry is probably referential... some sort of standards, like how you measure search[ability]' — a brand-side voice confirming the same fragmentation/standards gap from outside.

**Our angle:** We cannot fix this — we are not an ad-serving or orchestration platform and don't compete for retail-media budget, so 'de-fragmenting' walled gardens isn't our product. The honest angle is narrower: we can position as a neutral data/insight layer that doesn't add a new walled garden of its own, not as a solution to the fragmentation itself.

## 2. Selling into brands and retailers is a long, resource-gated cycle: vendors have to evangelize an unproven category before they can even get a meeting, then wait on client-side IT capacity, master-data readiness, and internal budget competition that has nothing to do with product quality.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It inflates customer-acquisition cost and stretches time-to-revenue independent of how good the technology is — deals stall not because the pitch fails but because the buyer's CRM is broken, their IT team is busy on something else, or their product data isn't clean enough to train the vendor's models.

**Self evidence:** Ben Reynolds (VP Business Development, Walkbase/STRATACACHE), ep. 215: 'the conversations four or five years ago were... why are you, why am I here? I had to evangelize. I had to convince somebody to even take a meeting. And then once I did, I had to convince them why they needed it. And then they had to go get budget for it.' / Paul Dahill (MD Sales EMEA, Koddi), ep. 307: 'as a vendor when you're working on retail media projects, this tends to be a kind of long sales process... you've got buy-in from above, but there are many competing questions for that resource... maybe the CRM also is a little broken... so perhaps that IT team is working on that.' / Peter Vasilyev (BD Director Europe, Ailet), ep. 153: 'it's the typical IT project, so need to allocate resources and understand the complexity, the geographical coverage — and in this case master data and product data are very important, and not all companies are ready to implement it.'

**Other-party evidence:** n/a — this theme is carried almost entirely by vendor BD/sales executives describing their own pipeline, which is itself notable.

**Our angle:** This is directly our own risk, not something we sell a fix for: we will face the identical friction selling into brands and retailers (champion-dependent, IT-gated, budget-competing). The honest angle is planning, not product — build the sales cycle assumptions accordingly and target buyers who already believe, rather than promising to solve this friction for other vendors.

## 3. There is a built-in conflict between showing clients transparent, incrementality-backed numbers and showing them numbers that look good — real measurement usually produces a worse-looking result than what was originally sold, and admitting that to a budget owner is commercially uncomfortable; meanwhile opaque fee-stacking in the programmatic chain means even sophisticated retailers often don't know what they're actually keeping.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It puts vendors in the position of either damaging their own credibility by delivering an unwelcome true number, or perpetuating the industry's trust problem by not doing so — and once clients do look closely at fee stacking, it directly threatens vendor revenue and retailer trust in the whole ecosystem.

**Self evidence:** Andreas Reiffen (CEO, Pentaleap), ep. 187: 'You either get transparent numbers or you see amazing results... if you see fully incrementality backed numbers they will not look as good as what was previously sold to you... sometimes you then have to go back to whoever is the budget owner and say hey, now as we know what the true return is it's actually a little bit less than what we thought it would be. And this is a difficult decision bias.' / Chris Riegel (CEO, STRATACACHE), ep. 212: 'we walk into retailers all the time and say, hey, you're in a fully programmatic environment, what are your total programmatic fees? And they literally have no idea... 58% of that is taken up by fees. There is no other world, no other dimension, no other galaxy in which a retailer is going to allow intermediaries to take better than half of that revenue.' / Jack Lindberg (Head of Product, Shalion), ep. 238, on running their own incrementality tooling for a client: 'we found something like 15% of the sales the retailer reported from ads were actually incremental. So a lot less than what you were getting.'

**Other-party evidence:** n/a for this specific tension — the transparency-vs-looking-good bind and the fee-opacity point are both told entirely in the vendor's own voice.

**Our angle:** This is the one place we could genuinely help, with real caveats. As an outside party with no ad-revenue stake in the numbers looking good, we could plausibly offer an honest, independent measurement or data layer that vendors themselves find awkward to deliver to their own clients. But this is a hard sell, not an easy one — no vendor invites third-party scrutiny of their own reported ROI eagerly, so 'trusted independent measurement' is a real angle, not a guaranteed one.

### Runners-up (failed the self-evidence test)

- Retailers hoard shopper data / brands can't get real access to retailer data — voiced almost entirely as a brand-side or industry-analyst complaint (Jacques Van Cauwenberge, Beko's Marco Brucato, the Nimble CEO describing retailers' lock-in to legacy providers); no data/measurement vendor described restricted retailer data access as their own commercial obstacle in first person.
- Commoditization and pricing pressure pushing data/measurement products toward a race to the bottom — 'race to the bottom' language appears repeatedly in the corpus, but always about brand pricing to consumers or retailer trade pricing (Rick Swinkels/TCC, Frederic Fernandez); no vendor executive described their own product pricing being commoditized.
- Lack of industry-wide measurement standardization (IAB in-store standards, digital shelf standards) — mostly narrated by standards-body staff (Marie-Clare Puffett) and brand execs (Henkel) rather than by vendors describing it as their own P&L problem; the one vendor mention (Ben Reynolds citing IAB standards) is folded into the ranked problems above rather than standing alone.

# RETAILERS (grocers, chains, marketplaces and their retail-media/commercial leadership)

**Self vs other:** Unlike the pattern the earlier pass warned about, this search turned up genuine, first-person self-evidence for retailers — but it is concentrated almost entirely on one topic: the internal organizational pain of standing up and running a retail-media business (Iceland, OBI, bol.com, Wolt all gave detailed, unprompted first-person accounts). That theme is self-evidence-rich. Everywhere else, the pattern from the prior corpus pass largely repeats: the richest, most quotable material on retailer margin pressure and supplier-relationship squeeze (the single most detailed episode in the whole search, 'Jacques Van Cauwenberge on EU Grocery Retail') is narrated by a 24-year Coca-Cola brand-side commercial executive now consulting to FMCGs, not by a retailer employee — pure OTHER-evidence about retailers that had to be excluded from ranking despite being the loudest signal in the corpus. Self-evidence on margin/cost-to-serve did exist, but only from smaller or non-traditional players (Jumia's marketplace CEO in Africa, Rohlik's international CEO, Samkaup's regional Icelandic co-op) — none of the large European grocery chains (Tesco, Carrefour, Ahold, REWE-as-a-whole, Aldi/Lidl) that dominate the 'margin pressure' headlines actually speak in their own voice about it anywhere in this corpus; when REWE and Sainsbury's executives do appear, they speak specifically as retail-media leads about retail media, not as grocery-P&L owners about grocery margin. So: roughly two-thirds of what surfaced under 'retailer problems' broadly was other-described (by brand-side FMCG executives, consultants, and ad-tech/agency vendors), and the one problem with robust, multi-company self-evidence is the internal build-out/stakeholder-alignment pain — which is exactly why it ranks #1.

## 1. Running a retail media business inside a retailer is an internal-organization problem more than a technology one — the retail-media/media-connect team has to constantly extract cooperation from category management, e-commerce, marketing and store-ops teams who have their own priorities, and has to fight for legitimacy as a small, different-shaped unit inside a much bigger traditional retail organization.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It burns the retail-media leader's own time and political capital (one head of retail media says he spends 80% of his energy just keeping internal peace instead of selling media), slows the transition that is supposed to be a new profit center, and puts the team's headcount/budget at constant risk because it has to keep re-justifying its existence to a company that is 'still' fundamentally a retailer.

**Self evidence:** Adam Smith, Head of Retail Media at Iceland: "the biggest challenge is the internal challenge... we work with the e-commerce team daily and we put them under pressure... we have to be very careful that we're not the sort of... bees nest that just irritates everybody in the business... you actually spend 80% of your time just keeping the thing going." (316. Adam Smith, Head of Retail Media at Iceland and Kiri Masters, 2026-03-28) — corroborated by Patricia Grundmann, VP Media & Retail Media at OBI: "The harder part is to do it internally... We are 50 people in retail media at OBI. OBI has over 40,000 employees... we are a minority in our company and we still need to like present our contribution to everything OBI is doing." (242. Patricia Grundmann, VP Media & Retail Media at OBI, 2025-05-24) — and Justin Sandee, Director Commercial Development at bol.com: "we want to profit from everything... at some point if you choose for media you need to talk that language of media as well... that's sometimes a little bit hard still to make that transition... that's the hardest part for a lot of companies." (331. Justin Sandee, Director Commercial Development at bol.com, 2026-07-04)

**Other-party evidence:** Sam Knights, CEO of SMG (retail-media agency, vendor to retailers), on retailers generally: "setting up a retail media network is not just having a piece of tech on your website. It involves structural change, it involves a lot of internal ambition and a lot of retailers I don't think have gone on that journey yet." (Sam Knights (SMG) and Ollie Shayer (Boots): The Golden Ticket of Retail Media, 2024-10-19)

**Our angle:** We cannot fix internal politics or organizational buy-in — that is a leadership and change-management problem no vendor can sell its way past. What we can honestly do is shrink the day-to-day operational load our specific slice touches: our screen-content system is built to run largely unattended (precomputed prompts/templates, automated targeting, no per-campaign manual configuration), so it doesn't add to the 'putting marketing and e-commerce under pressure' load these teams already describe — but it does not make category management say yes faster.

## 2. In e-commerce and quick-commerce, growth and profitability pull in opposite directions unless the underlying cost structure is fixed first — several retail/marketplace CEOs describe having to run extremely lean on logistics, fulfillment and G&A, and to deliberately hold back from over-monetizing suppliers, just to keep a positive margin per order at all.

**Why it hurts (medium confidence):** It caps how fast they can chase growth (Rohlik's own CEO: grow without fixing the model and 'the more you grow, more you lose'), and it constrains commercial upside with suppliers (Jumia's CEO: 'we cannot over monetize vendors... we're a bit constrained on the margin'), meaning management has to defend thin, hard-won margins rather than simply chase top-line growth or trade-term leverage.

**Self evidence:** Francis Dufay, CEO at Jumia: "we cannot over monetize vendors either. We're not like Alibaba in China or Amazon in the US where everyone wants to work with you... we're a bit constrained on the margin, but it means we have to be extremely lean and extremely efficient in everything we do below gross profit." (221. Francis Dufay, CEO at Jumia: Digital Retail in Africa, 2025-02-01) — and Olin Novák, CEO International at Rohlik Group: "you need to make it profitable because if not, then like more you grow, more you lose. And the more problem, the more the structural problems become an issue." (340. Olin Novák, CEO International at Rohlik Group, 2026-08-05)

**Other-party evidence:** Rob Manhoudt, VP Digital Commerce Europe at Procter & Gamble (brand side, describing the retailer/platform side of the equation): "Very often, it is all about logistical costs, operating costs, delivery costs, inbound costs, outbound costs, inventory, cash flow. That is often online where the cost structure is most heavy." (326. Rob Manhoudt, VP Digital Commerce Europe at Procter & Gamble, 2026-06-03)

**Our angle:** We cannot touch this problem directly — it's a logistics/fulfillment/G&A cost-structure issue in e-commerce delivery economics, entirely outside what an in-store screen and content-personalization platform does. The closest honest connection is that a physical retailer with stores (unlike a pure e-commerce marketplace) can use our platform to turn existing screen real estate into an incremental, low-capex margin line — but that doesn't solve the delivery-cost problem these two executives are actually describing, and we should not claim it does.

## 3. Small and regional retailers are structurally disadvantaged on buying power and supply terms versus large national/international chains — they get worse wholesaler pricing, get turned away or mocked by big brands for order sizes too small to bother with, and are stuck running some stores at a loss purely to keep a community served.

**Why it hurts (medium confidence):** It directly erodes their price competitiveness against larger rivals entering their market (e.g. Costco), forces reliance on a wholesale middleman layer that inflates shelf prices, and means some of their own stores are known money-losers kept open for non-commercial reasons — a real commercial drag with no easy internal fix since it's a function of scale, not execution.

**Self evidence:** Gunnur Gunnarsdóttir, Executive Director of Retail Operations at Samkaup (Iceland): "with one store and one co-op you don't have the buying power to be able to sell this products... when we have been going out in shows and trying to get some brands they laughed us when we are just maybe it's one unit we need, but they're like we're not going to ship that to Iceland just for one unit... it's not always a good investment, but it's like this bigger purpose of it to be giving back to the community." (Gunnur Gunnarsdóttir, Executive Director of Retail Operations and Human Resources at Samkaup: Retail in Iceland, 2025-02-19)

**Other-party evidence:** Andreas Reiffen, CEO at Pentaleap (ad-tech vendor to retailers), on retailers generally: "it's so crucial for retailers to bring additional margin in to play that game against companies like Amazon that what you pay for tech matters a lot because it immediately impacts your EBITDA margin... the overarching problem is how to get scale." (265. The Big Retail Media Ad-Tech Talk with Andreas Reiffen, 2025-09-10)

**Our angle:** We cannot address this at all — it is a procurement/wholesale-leverage and market-density problem that has nothing to do with in-store screen content or AI-personalized advertising, and no software layer we build changes a small retailer's negotiating weight with global FMCG suppliers.

# Brands (FMCG manufacturers and their commercial/marketing/e-commerce leadership)

**Self vs other:** A large share of the vivid "brand pain" language in this corpus is other-sourced: vendor CEOs and sales leaders (NielsenIQ, PunttAI, Pentaleap, Zitcha, Koddi, TCC Global, Stacklinq, Path to Purchase Institute) describe brand struggles empathetically as the setup for their own pitch, and podcast hosts frequently recite third-party survey findings (the Path to Purchase Institute's annual RMN survey) to brand guests as a prompt rather than the guests raising it unprompted. When strictly narrowed to a brand-side executive describing their OWN problem in their own words, the corpus does support three distinct, repeated themes across different companies and different years (Keurig Dr Pepper, PepsiCo, Henkel, L'Oréal, Kellanova, Colgate-Palmolive, General Mills) — proving marketing/media ROI, fragmented data, and organizational strain building new capability. Several other loud, frequently-repeated corpus themes (retailer power imbalance, private-label pressure, retail-media fragmentation as its own headline, content-production bottlenecks, consumer-trust erosion) failed the self-evidence bar and were demoted to runners-up despite appearing often — they are corpus-wide narratives told mostly by people selling the fix or observing from outside the brand seat, echoing exactly the risk this assignment was designed to catch.

## 1. We cannot prove the ROI / effectiveness of what we spend (marketing, media, retail media) — leadership keeps asking for the link between spend and results and there is no clean, trusted way to show it.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It is the single most-cited internal pressure point: it determines whether a marketer's budget survives the next cycle, whether they get taken seriously by finance/leadership, and whether the CMO has a seat in decisions. Several execs describe it as a permanent, unresolved fight inside their own company, not a one-off project.

**Self evidence:** Richard Rodgers, Marketing Director at Keurig Dr Pepper: 'I've been very opinionated as well within our own company because of course that's something that everybody's always going to ask you. What was the ROI for that investment?' and, on his own retail-media partners: 'they're very much aware that we can't get the clear reporting and the KPIs that we are essentially looking for.' Sorin Patilinet, marketing effectiveness lead at PepsiCo: 'the push to prove the impact, the ROI, the sales impact, the share price impact of your activities have been higher than ever. And there's no clear solution. There's no like magic bullet that everybody uses.' He also names the meta-problem: brand practitioners themselves are the quietest voice in the industry conversation about their own effectiveness ('there's very little incentive for a brand to speak up out there').

**Other-party evidence:** Nearly every retail-media-network guest and podcast host recites the Path to Purchase Institute's annual survey of CPG brands (minimum spend requirements, lack of data sharing, closed-loop reporting, 'lack of ability to prove ROI') to brand guests as a prompt — that specific catalogue is third-party/survey-sourced, not independently volunteered, though the guests then affirm it is their lived experience too. Vendors (Koddi, Zitcha/Pentaleap, Path to Purchase Institute analysts) describe brands as 'annoyed' and RMN measurement as 'chaos' from the outside.

**Our angle:** We cannot fix cross-network media/marketing ROI attribution or MMM-grade effectiveness proof — that is a different product category (marketing mix modeling, incrementality testing across dozens of retail media networks) and not what Minds & Models builds. At most our in-store screen analytics could someday be one clean input into a brand's broader effectiveness stack, but we do not solve the CFO-facing 'prove it' problem they're describing.

## 2. Our data lives in disconnected silos (online vs. offline, retailer by retailer, tool by tool) and it's a huge, ongoing effort just to get it into a structure we can act on — so digital/commerce insight ends up sidelined in the business, not integrated into it.

**Why it hurts (high confidence):** It means the same KPI (market share, execution) gets reported two different ways, digital data 'always gets looked at at the very end' of business reviews because there's no time for something that isn't integrated, and every AI/agentic ambition stalls on the same unbuilt foundation.

**Self evidence:** Boris Rütten, Global Head of eCommerce at Henkel: 'I was used to structure data before... super fragmented all the different data sources and it was a huge amount of effort to get the data in a similar structure as I was used to in an offline world... This is hard work because it wasn't simply available off the shelf... You have your online dashboard, your offline dashboard, and you look at it in a different way... the digital commerce part will always be looked at at the very end and then there's no time on the agenda because it's not integrated.' Beyza Kapu, Global Ecom Tech & Data Analytics Director at L'Oréal, on why agentic/AI initiatives stall: 'companies treat this as a silo, a marketing project... a tech project, an innovation topic. But it touches everything, data, content, tech, governance, and organizational design... there's also one other thing that keeps me awake at night.'

**Other-party evidence:** Salim Bachatene, SVP eCommerce Sales at NielsenIQ (vendor), describing the brand-side experience empathetically: 'I always put myself in the shoes of an e-com person like having to toggle between different solutions, different data sets, to create an insight to report back or to create an action — and it's horrible.'

**Our angle:** This is the closest to our wheelhouse. Minds & Models' dashboard is built to be a single, integrated source of truth for the screen/in-store analytics layer, so we can plausibly remove the 'toggle between dashboards' pain for that specific slice of a brand's data — but we cannot unify a brand's entire online+offline, retailer-by-retailer data estate; that's a much bigger enterprise data problem than one product solves.

## 3. Standing up a new capability (e-commerce, digital commerce, retail media) inside a legacy FMCG organization is a genuine organizational strain — resourcing, IT infrastructure, supply chain retooling, and finding/training people who have skills the old org chart never needed (media literacy on the sales side, curiosity over pedigree in hiring).

**Why it hurts (medium confidence):** It's not a one-time project cost — it's an ongoing tax on execution speed, and it puts commercial/sales people into media negotiations they were never trained for, creating visible confusion in front of retailers ('people are looking at each other like who's responding to which question').

**Self evidence:** Christoph Sterkel, Head of eCommerce Northern Europe at Kellanova: 'there were like probably four major challenges we had to overcome. So first was team resources and organization. The second would be all around IT infrastructure... content and portfolio. And finally, supply chain and logistics' — illustrated with Kellanova's own warehouse needing to be rebuilt for pick-and-pack because it was built for full truckloads. Vivek Rastogi, Senior Director Global Digital Commerce at Colgate-Palmolive: 'a lot of times people who are on the sales side of things... have to talk media suddenly. And they've never spoken media. They don't understand advertising... they're suddenly sitting in a meeting with a retailer like an Amazon or a Walmart... it's a huge gap suddenly.' Shri Raja Gopalan (General Mills, formerly PepsiCo), on hiring for e-commerce: 'finding people who are intellectually curious is not as easy as it seems to the eye. They're a very few and a rare breed.'

**Other-party evidence:** Consultants and recruiters (Kearney's Siddarth Pathak, People & Culture execs) generalize about FMCG needing 'a different mindset' to hire and retain digital talent industry-wide.

**Our angle:** We cannot fix this — headcount, hiring, internal reorganization and supply-chain retooling are organizational-design problems outside what a software product can solve. At best, a genuinely simple, low-code dashboard lowers the technical bar to operate the analytics layer we provide, but the core pain (finding hybrid talent, rebuilding infrastructure) is not ours to address.

# Incumbents: NielsenIQ deep read

Three episodes, one NIQ executive: Salim Bachatene, SVP eCommerce Sales at NielsenIQ, appearing as a recurring co-host across ep 309 (with Alex Juday, Incremental), ep 315 (with Beyza Kapu, L'Oréal) and ep 320 (with Boris Rütten, Henkel). Read in full, not searched. The dominant finding is that this is not a measurement conversation at all — across ~250 chunks NIQ's senior seller never once defends or describes a measurement product. He describes a *plumbing* problem: unstructured, unmatched, non-cross-referenceable commerce data, with no industry standards, which he says the industry lacks and which he does not claim NIQ can fix alone ("in my point of view what we lack in the industry is probably referential... I don't know who can do that"). He concedes he is not an incrementality expert, defers all incrementality methodology to a competitor vendor on the same panel, and closes every episode on "structured and centralized" data readiness rather than any NIQ analytic capability. The brand guests do push back, and both push back on data they own, not on NIQ: Henkel's Boris Rütten states flatly, on air, that Henkel has NOT harmonised its data ("I be honest we have not. It's too much"), and L'Oréal's Beyza Kapu states that the metric she actually needs — whether a consumer trusts and follows a recommendation — "today we're not capable of measuring it." Crucially for us: the entire three-episode arc is digital-shelf, PDP and LLM-legibility. ## Findings

### [HIGH] NIQ's own SVP says the industry has no commerce-data standards and openly does not know who can set them — the single most exploitable concession in the three episodes.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene, SVP eCommerce Sales, NielsenIQ (ep 320, Henkel): "in my point of view what we lack in the industry is probably referential Like you know I don't know who can do that but some sort of standards like how you measure search What is the minimum viable way of measuring search What is the minimum viable way of measuring availability Same for content Same for sales". And again later in the same episode, answering a direct question about working without digital shelf standards: "so there are no standards in the industry today". In ep 309 he framed the same thing as an aspiration: "my my kind of dream is that we start to create an industry standardization of what is required... I think in retail media, proper retail media, there is like the IAB kind of standards on how to measure some of the things. But I think in digital shelf, we didn't reach this." TYPE: SELF (vendor executive describing the limits of his own industry and, implicitly, his own firm).

**So what:** The incumbent is publicly conceding that the measurement definitions in the layer adjacent to ours are unsettled and ownerless. A method-first supplier that arrives with a defensible, published definition of what it measures and at what granularity/frequency is walking into an acknowledged vacuum, not competing against a settled standard. This is the strongest single hook for the data-brokers pillar.

### [HIGH] A brand executive concedes, unprompted and on air in front of NIQ, that his company has NOT harmonised its data across silos — the highest-value buyer admission in the three episodes.

**Evidence:** Boris Rütten, Global Head of eCommerce, Henkel Consumer Brands (ep 320), answering an audience question from Troy Townson about normalising output across media/merchandise/marketing silos: "It's it's it's difficult and I be honest we have not. It's too much. We we have not harmonized all data from all those different parts." Earlier in the same episode he describes the operational consequence: "You have your online dashboard your offline dashboard and and you look at it in a different way You have business reviews because of the different dashboards like the digital commerce part will always be looked at at the very end and then there's no time on the agenda because it's not integrated." And his named biggest failure: "we kept adding data sources but we didn't take care of the governance enough of the existing data source because things change. So, what happens? You create a super complexity. You have a lot of data, you have your dashboards and then you start getting the complaints from the end user because they look at the data and they keep sending you messages. Okay, this cannot be true. This is wrong." TYPE: SELF (brand executive on his own company's problem).

**So what:** This is the buyer describing his own pain in his own words, which is exactly what the prior pass over this corpus lacked. It also tells us the failure mode to avoid: 'let's add a data source' is what he says burned him. Any pitch that positions us as an additional feed into an existing data lake is pitching into his documented scar tissue. We must arrive as an answer to a named use case with its own governance story, never as another source.

### [HIGH] A brand executive names a metric she needs that nobody can measure — and it is a who/why metric, not a what metric.

**Evidence:** Beyza Kapu, Global eCom Tech & Data Analytics Director, L'Oréal (ep 315): "the next real metric today we're not capable of measuring it, but we should consider measuring is the share of trust because recommendation only matters if people trust the system enough to follow it, right? But indeed it is not practical today. Today we're barely managing the share of conversation and share of recommendation." She also names her own portfolio problem: "our portfolios are large, fragmented, multi-market, multi-retailer, and often inconsistent in how product data and content are managed" and the stake: "there's also a new risk which is risk of invisibility because if you're not in the answer, you are effectively not in the market. And that's much harsher form of I would say competitive exclusion." TYPE: SELF (brand executive on her own company and her own unmet measurement need).

**So what:** A senior brand data leader stating on record that the measurement she wants does not exist, immediately after the incumbent's seller has described his offer as 'structured and centralized data'. The gap she names is behavioural and attitudinal — did the person actually act on it — which is the shape of what an observation layer answers. Note she frames it as an AI-mediation problem, not a store problem; we would have to bridge that framing ourselves.

### [HIGH] NIQ's seller disclaims incrementality expertise and hands the entire methodology conversation to a competing vendor on the same panel.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene (ep 309), in the opening minutes of an episode titled "Incrementality, Myth or Reality?": "I kind of agree with everything you said, Alex. So first of all, I'm not an incrementality expert, but I think I got dragged into this conversation in the last 12 to 18 months. ... And the reason is like, you know, if you want to do incrementality analysis, you have you need data. And I think that's how I so my expertise is around data." For the rest of the episode every methodological question (ensemble models, passive experimentation, difference-in-difference, MMM cadence) is answered by Alex Juday of Incremental, not by NIQ. TYPE: SELF (vendor executive on the boundary of his own competence and remit).

**So what:** NIQ, in this corpus, is not positioning as an attribution or causality company. It is positioning as a data-supply and data-structure company. That means we are not competing with NIQ for the incrementality budget — and it means the natural relationship to NIQ is supplier/partner (a source they cross-reference), not rival. It also means any pitch to NIQ should be about data assets and coverage, not about analytics or lift methodology.

### [HIGH] NIQ explicitly frames its value as cross-referencing many sources — and lists 'shopper behavior' as one of them, without describing where that comes from. That is the named attach point for a partner pitch.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene (ep 309): "the quality of the insight or the action that you can take is materially different based on the amount of data you use, not the amount in terms of like one silo, but the more you cross-reference different sources of data. So sales, market share, digital self signals, shopper behavior, um all these sources on retailer data, some data coming from like you know, more of um marketing and media at that point." And in ep 320 on the sequencing of what they built: "our response has always been we strongly believe that the integration and the correlation is the future So next step of correlation for digital shelf is sales data... And then the next level is you bring more data sets like shopper behavior you know sometimes some internal data like supply chain data some finance data and so on so forth And the more that you enrich the the more you can do". TYPE: SELF (vendor describing his own firm's strategy and roadmap).

**So what:** He states the roadmap out loud: digital shelf → sales → shopper behaviour. Shopper behaviour is the next enrichment layer they want and the only one in the list he never says they have solved. That is precisely the slot a shopper-observation layer occupies. A data-brokers pillar pitch should quote this back to him verbatim and offer to fill the named next step, framed as an enrichment source that is already structured and matchable.

### [HIGH] The incumbent's whole doctrine is 'reverse the pyramid, start from the use case, start small' — which is a direct instruction for how to sell to and through them, and a warning against selling a dataset.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene, repeated in both ep 309 and ep 320: "I've seen a lot of manufacturers tackling the pyramid from the wrong side which is let me buy some data, then let me that data in my data lake... and then one that is done with all the hurdles that come, the last stage is like okay, what do we do now? I think that we should inverse completely the pyramid and we should start like what do I need to do? What are the 10 20 30 use cases that I'm trying to solve? And from there we start okay, so who do I bring? What are the partners that I need to bring around my table? Then once we design that like okay, what kind of data do I need?" And: "the standards become a response to what you're trying to achieve um as actions in in your daily daily work." Boris Rütten independently arrives at the same rule from the buyer side: "my main learning is um not to start with the data because we have more data available than we can use. It start with the application what kind of insights you want to look at and then be specific on the data sources". TYPE: SELF on both sides (vendor and brand, converging).

**So what:** Both the incumbent and the brand buyer say, independently, that a data-first pitch fails and a use-case-first pitch wins. Our deck must open with two or three named decisions a client makes differently, then reveal the observation layer as the minimum data required to make them — never open with what our sensors capture. This also means that a standard we publish is only credible if it is derived from a use case, which is exactly how Salim says standards should be built.

### [MEDIUM] The brand-side voice on the panel rejects 'more data' outright and reframes the need as penetration/frequency/trip questions — which is who-layer language, and simultaneously the strongest argument against selling us as a dataset.

**Evidence:** Efraín Rosario, co-host and FMCG veteran, explicitly speaking "putting my my brand hat on" (ep 309), adding a fourth mountain to Incremental's three: "we can have all the data in the world, but I can tell you that my for my perspective as somebody who's been in this industry for quite a while, I don't think we lack I don't think lack of data is our problem. I think turning that data into actual insight or implications around the so what, like what I was saying what do you go do with it is actually the problem." And what he says he actually wants to know: "are we talking incrementality in terms of like am I driving buyer penetration? Am I driving shopping trip penetration or incidents? Am I driving incrementality through average spend? Am I driving it through frequency of purchase? That's the things I want to know. And the reason that I as a brand want to know that is because those are the things that I can start the action." TYPE: SELF-ADJACENT — he is a podcast host at Dwyer Partners, not a serving brand executive, but speaking from named prior brand experience and explicitly in a brand persona. Treat as strong colour, not as a clean buyer datapoint.

**So what:** Two-edged. The question list (penetration, trip incidence, frequency) is a who-layer question set and gives us the vocabulary a brand actually uses. But his headline — lack of data is not the problem — is the objection our entire category will meet in the first meeting. We should pre-empt it explicitly: we are not adding a dataset, we are answering three questions that the client currently answers by assumption.

### [MEDIUM] The competing vendor on ep 309 states that the complete cross-channel individual-level view will probably never be built, because the incentives of the parties who would have to collaborate do not align.

**Evidence:** Alex Juday, SVP eCommerce Revenue, Incremental (ep 309): "I'm not sure we we ever get to the like perfect solution where someone can actually follow Daniel around the internet everywhere and understand if he was in a Walmart or not in Walmart and in a CVS and then bought it on TikTok. I don't think I don't know if we'll ever ever get there, right? I don't think there's speaking of incentives, the right incentives for the players that would need to collaborate to do that." And on why his company exists at all: "most retailers don't enable brands or agencies to run geo-base lift studies or RCTs. If they did uh that would be fantastic. I I probably wouldn't have a job, but I it would be a lot easier, right? If they allowed you to do those things. They don't." TYPE: SELF (vendor on the structural limits of his own market and the contingency of his own business).

**So what:** A vendor admitting the omniscient panel will never be assembled is the strongest available argument for a bounded, physically-grounded observation layer: we do not promise to follow the shopper everywhere, we promise to observe one moment completely. It also tells us the retailer is the gatekeeper who withholds the experiment — which supports selling the observation layer to the retailer as an asset they can then offer brands, rather than selling around them.

### [MEDIUM] The only critique of the incumbents' data comes from a competitor vendor and from podcast hosts — never from a brand. This is exactly the trap the previous pass fell into.

**Evidence:** Alex Juday, Incremental (ep 309): "I think most traditional measurement providers uh lack the right actual data to do it. Then they probably lack the actual infrastructure that even if they had the data to allow themselves to do it. Or then you have some people who use very oversimplified restatements of ROAS. That's not incrementality." Also his marketing statistic, used unchallenged: "we see on average brands have up to 42% of their budget that exist today in the wrong place simply because ROAS has been driving this last portions of activation." On MMMs: "we we know they fall short in regards to the speed and granularity that practitioners need". Separately, on syndicated latency, from CPG Guys hosts (ex-industry, not serving brand executives): "new items that may take time to show up in your syndicated data reports that you're very used to and even if you have a field sales force by the time I reported, it may take 10 days or a week. I haven't seen anyone to the day including DSD successfully capture in store intelligence from the field force in any meaningful manner". TYPE: OTHER in every case (vendor on rival vendors; hosts on the category).

**So what:** Do not build a competitive slide out of these. The '42% of budget in the wrong place' number and 'traditional providers lack the right data' are both seller claims from a firm whose product is the alternative. If we recycle them we are shipping a competitor's marketing as market evidence. Use them only as evidence that the incumbent-critique narrative exists in the market — never as proof it is true.

### [MEDIUM] NIQ's seller will not criticise brands on the record, and both brand guests are treated as partners, not accounts. The relationship on display is advisory and long-standing.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene (ep 309), asked directly "what are they doing wrong?": "Um I so no I won't comment on what they are doing but like on on what can be done." On L'Oréal (ep 315): "before you answered, these are the conversations we have with Beza for more than a year now." On Henkel (ep 320): "I think Boris was the first person ever that challenged us on on the data... I remember at the time no one was caring about this like the granularity the cleanness the the fact that the data was structured and these were like super interesting conversations we were having six years ago." TYPE: SELF (vendor describing his own go-to-market posture).

**So what:** NIQ's account relationships at L'Oréal and Henkel are multi-year, co-narrated, and publicly warm. There is no wedge to drive between NIQ and these brands, and attempting one would be read as gauche. If we approach NIQ it must be as a complementary source into an account they already own; if we approach these brands it must be on a question NIQ is not in the room for.

### [MEDIUM] NIQ builds rather than buys, over long horizons, and its stated end-state is consolidation of the client's data into one structured place — a frame that is hostile to an independent third layer.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene (ep 320): "we spent years and years trying to solve this challenge and we did"; "we started using AI for this specific case 10 years ago. And you know on the back end of course we developed specific models and it was very hard"; and on the categorisation problem "at that time no one was doing it and and I think still today not a lot of players are able to do it." His closing recommendation to brands (ep 315): "As you can imagine with my role, data. So I would say data and two keywords structured and centralized." TYPE: SELF.

**So what:** This is the argument AGAINST approaching NIQ as a customer. Their instinct on a hard data problem is a decade-long in-house build, and their sales narrative is centralisation — one structured source, theirs. An independent observation layer reads to them as a component to acquire or replicate, not a partner to license. If we do approach them, the only durable position is as a source of a signal they physically cannot collect (in-store observation of people, not products) and would not build a hardware estate to get.

### [MEDIUM] NIQ's seller concedes an inherent speed-versus-accuracy tradeoff in commerce data, and concedes the work is unfinished.

**Evidence:** Salim Bachatene (ep 320): "you have two variables of the same equation, speed versus cleanness if I may say. So, if you increase the speed you might lose a little bit of cleanness." And on the maturity of what NIQ has built: "I don't know I don't think we achieve the full potential of it yet. I think there is yet a lot to do." And on the end user's daily reality: "I always put myself in the shoes of an e-com person like having to toggle between different solutions different data sets to create an insight to report back or to create an action and it's horrible." TYPE: SELF.

**So what:** 'It's horrible' is the incumbent describing the end-user experience of the current toolset — usable in a pitch as the incumbent's own words. And the speed/cleanness tradeoff is a real technical concession we can position against: an observation layer that is clean at the moment of capture does not have to trade one for the other.

## Positioning implications

- Position alongside, not against. Across three episodes NIQ's most senior eCommerce seller never claims causality, attribution, incrementality or people-measurement; he claims data structure, matching, coverage and cross-referencing. There is no head-on collision. Anything we write that frames NIQ as the incumbent we are displacing is fighting a battle they are not in, and will read as uninformed to anyone who has heard these episodes.
- The strongest attach line for the data-brokers pillar is his own roadmap, verbatim: digital shelf → sales data → 'and then the next level is you bring more data sets like shopper behavior'. Open a partnership approach by quoting that sentence and saying we are the shopper-behaviour layer, already structured and matchable to the identifiers they already carry. This is his framing, not ours, which makes it much harder to dismiss.
- Lead with a standard, not a dataset. He says twice that the industry lacks a 'referential' and that he does not know who can create one, and that 'the standard becomes a response to what you're trying to achieve as actions'. A published, use-case-derived definition of what we measure at what frequency and granularity is not a nice-to-have — it is the exact artefact he says is missing. Ship it as a document, not as a spec sheet.
- Structure every deck use-case-first. Both the incumbent (Salim: 'inverse completely the pyramid... what are the 10 20 30 use cases') and the buyer (Boris: 'not to start with the data... start with the application') independently say data-first pitches fail. Our first three slides must be three decisions the client currently makes on assumption; the observation layer appears only as the minimum data required to make them differently.
- Pre-empt 'we don't lack data' explicitly. Efraín Rosario in brand persona is blunt: 'I don't think lack of data is our problem.' Boris Rütten's named biggest failure was adding data sources without governance. Our category's default pitch — here is a new signal — lands directly on both scars. Answer it on slide two: we are not a source, we are an answer to a question you currently answer by assumption, and here is the governance model that comes with it.
- Use the brand vocabulary the corpus gives us. Efraín's four words are buyer penetration, trip penetration/incidence, average spend, frequency of purchase. Map our outputs onto those, not onto detection categories. Beyza Kapu adds 'contextual relevance — products need to be defined not only by what they are, but what they solve, for whom, in which situations'. 'For whom, in which situations' is a who-layer brief written by a brand executive.
- ARGUMENT AGAINST APPROACHING NIQ AS A CUSTOMER (take seriously): they build, over decade-long horizons, and their sales narrative is consolidation. 'we spent years and years trying to solve this challenge and we did'; 'we started using AI for this specific case 10 years ago'; and the closing recommendation to brands is 'data and two keywords structured and centralized' — i.e. one place, theirs. An independent third layer reads to that organisation as a component to replicate or acquire cheaply, not a partner to license richly. If we approach them, the only defensible position is a signal they physically cannot collect without a hardware estate and would not build one to get. Do not approach them with anything a smart team could scrape or model.
- SECOND ARGUMENT AGAINST: their brand accounts are co-narrated and multi-year. Salim publicly describes six years of conversations with Henkel and over a year with L'Oréal, and refuses on air to say anything critical about brands ('I won't comment on what they are doing'). There is no wedge available between NIQ and these accounts, and trying to create one would be conspicuous in a small, highly-networked European FMCG community that listens to this podcast.
- Do NOT recycle Incremental's competitive claims. 'most traditional measurement providers lack the right actual data to do it' and 'brands have up to 42% of their budget in the wrong place' are both from Alex Juday, SVP eCommerce Revenue at a firm selling the alternative. Putting either in our materials would repeat the exact error the previous corpus pass made: shipping vendor marketing as buyer evidence. If we want an incumbent critique, we currently do not have a buyer-voiced one and should say so internally rather than borrow.
- The retailer, not the brand, is the gatekeeper of the clean experiment — and Alex Juday says so from a position of self-interest against it: 'most retailers don't enable brands or agencies to run geo-base lift studies or RCTs. If they did that would be fantastic. I probably wouldn't have a job.' This supports selling the observation layer to the retailer as an asset they can offer brands, rather than selling to brands and then needing retailer permission. It also means a retailer that grants observation access gains something brands currently cannot buy from anyone.
- Honest caveat that should reach whoever writes the deck: this corpus does not contain a brand executive complaining that syndicated measurement fails to tell them who the shopper is. The problem we sell into is not voiced here by the buyer. What IS voiced by buyers is (a) unharmonised data and dashboard silos (Henkel, self), (b) a needed metric that cannot be measured (L'Oréal, self), and (c) praise for retailer-side who-data from dunnhumby and Nectar 360 (Kimberly-Clark, self — 'they're really helping us to go the next step to actually really truly understand our shoppers'). That last one is a warning: in the UK at least, a brand already believes the retail media networks are solving the who question. Our positioning has to say what those data do not cover — the unidentified, non-loyalty-card, in-front-of-the-fixture shopper — rather than claim the who-question is unanswered.
