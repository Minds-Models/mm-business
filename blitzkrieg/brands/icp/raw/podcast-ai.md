# Agentic commerce and the AI-intermediated shopper — what "agentic" actually means to operators vs. vendors, how near each side thinks it is, and whether it makes physical shopper observation more or less valuable

"Agentic" (84 chunks/21 episodes) and the narrower "agentic commerce" (18 chunks/3 episodes, verified exact-match) is the corpus's biggest AI theme, but it splits cleanly by who's talking. Vendors selling AI/data/commerce tooling (NielsenIQ, Commerce/BigCommerce, AWS, Threedium, Nimble, PunttAI, StrataCache, Botify) treat it as urgent and largely already happening. Operators are far more mixed: L'Oréal's e-commerce director gives the most careful definition and calls it a real "human behavior shift," but Danone's global sales director explicitly calls it hype ("still for the future... 90% of your sales are done here and now") and Chalhoub Group's Chief Omnichannel and AI Officer says flatly "I don't think anybody's solved for it," treating agentic as one buzzy bet among many he's "being cautious" about. What breaks, per both camps: products must become "legible to a machine" (L'Oréal), the funnel collapses, retail media's on-site targeting signals erode (NielsenIQ/independent retail-media consultant), and PDP/structured data becomes "the new packaging." One vendor (Botify's CRO, backed by a retail-research CEO) specifically flags grocery/FMCG as the category most exposed, because routine reorders ("80% of what goes in the basket is the same as last week") are the easiest thing to hand to an agent — a direct risk to the categories a Czech/Slovak in-store measurement business would serve. On the central question — does buying moving to agents make observing the physical shopper more or less valuable — the corpus is silent: no operator or vendor frames that tradeoff at all. The closest adjacent facts are that two operators (Iceland, Danone) independently report 60–90% of sales still happen physically today, and one in-store-tech vendor (StrataCache) argues in-store retail media is comparatively insulated from AI disruption versus online — useful raw material, but not evidence for the "last unmediated human signal" thesis itself.

## Findings

### [operator / high] Operators and vendors converge on a three-layer definition of agentic commerce (AI-assisted → AI-mediated → agentic/autonomous-acting), most fully articulated by an operator.

Beyza Kapu (L'Oréal, Global Ecom Tech & Data Analytics Director), ep315: 'The first layer I would say AI assisted commerce... The second layer I would say AI mediated commerce... And the third layer is what we call now agentic commerce. This is when the system doesn't only interpret and recommend, but actually begins to act — shortlisting, bundling, automating, reordering, initiating steps. And eventually at one stage perhaps purchasing on consumer's behalf.'

### [vendor / high] Vendors selling AI/commerce infrastructure describe agentic commerce as urgent and already in motion, not a future bet.

Sharon Gee (SVP Product AI, Commerce/BigCommerce), ep303: 'Data is the storefront, customers the channel, agents are customers too... this is my mantra'; and on timing, 'How far do you think we're from that? ... months... It's happening now in many cases.' Also Mike Charalambous (Co-CEO, Threedium), ep343: 'for the first time ever as a company... we're now playing Champions League' working on agent-to-agent product discovery.

### [operator / high] Some senior operators explicitly push back on agentic urgency as overstated hype relative to where the business actually is today.

Camille Lavenant (Global Sales and Category Director, Danone), ep344: 'when you leave the e-commerce bubble... unfortunately a little bit back to well okay yes we need... AI, agentic etc. but it's still for the future... you still have 90% of your sales that are done here and now.' Amit Keswani (Chief Omnichannel and AI Officer, Chalhoub Group), ep318: 'The agentic piece... is very buzzy right now. I don't think... anybody's solved for it and there's everybody saying they can solve it, but if you go and look at it... they're not really solving for it yet... we're being cautious about everything agentic.'

### [vendor / high] Vendors flag routine/repeat FMCG grocery purchasing as the category most exposed to near-term agentic disruption — a direct risk to the category base a CZ/SK in-store measurement business would serve.

Charley Hackerson (CRO, Botify), ep246: 'I think that's going to be the first one to get really hit by agentic shopping where I have my own personal shopping agent that is going to do repetitive purchases for me... because an agent buys very differently than a user.' Richard Lim (CEO, Retail Economics), same episode: 'It's those routine purchases where consumers know where they're going to shop. 80% of the time, 80% of what goes in their basket is the same thing that they put in the basket last week.'

### [operator / high] Two independent operators report that the large majority of purchase volume remains physically in-store today, with no near-term expectation of that flipping.

Adam Smith (Head of Retail Media, Iceland), ep316: 'Iceland is 20% online... with 75 percent of the sales of our business still come through the shop... our opportunity still is in store.' Camille Lavenant (Danone), ep344: '60% 70%, it depends, will be still offline.'

### [vendor / medium] One vendor whose product is directly analogous to in-store shopper-observation (in-store screens + tracking) tells its own market that in-store is comparatively insulated from AI/agentic disruption versus online — a self-interested but usable claim.

StrataCache (Tijmen Willems), ep304: 'the impact on retail media online will be much much bigger, right?... when it comes to in store retail media where still the majority of purchases are being made, I think what you can use AI for is... creative.'

### [absent / high] The corpus does not contain anyone — operator or vendor — framing the specific tradeoff of whether physical-shopper observation becomes more or less valuable once AI intermediates buying decisions.

Targeted searches ('physical store shopper observation agentic AI', 'last unmediated human decision signal in store camera computer vision', 'does in-store data become more valuable when purchasing moves online or to AI') across the full corpus surfaced adjacent material (in-store retail-media measurement pitches, digital-shelf discussion) but no guest posed or answered this specific question in either direction.

### [mixed / high] "Digital self"/structured product data (PDP completeness, taxonomy, machine-readable claims) is repeatedly named — by both camps — as the new packaging/shelf battleground, but the entire framing is about winning machine legibility online, not about capturing physical shopper behavior.

Beyza Kapu (L'Oréal, operator): 'your product has to be understood, trusted, and recommendable by a system, not just recognized by consumer... the agentic commerce introduces another audience in our world, machines.' Salim Bachatene (SVP, NielsenIQ, vendor): 'Feeding an LLM with contradictory data is basically what kills the LLM recommendations... there will be a need of more harmonization.'

### [vendor / medium] A packaging-AI vendor argues visual packaging still matters because LLMs 'judge books by their cover,' but hedges his own claim, admitting nobody actually knows what drives LLM product recommendations yet.

Ronnie Kwesi Coleman (CEO, PunttAI), ep338: 'if anybody promises you that they know how to get you to be number one in an LLM is lying because... people don't fully know how LLMs work... my point is not that you should just use our approach... nobody is going to know what's going to be the thing that will make their brand stand out.'

### [vendor / high] The most totalizing vendor claim in the corpus — that brands no longer have a real relationship with consumers, only platforms do — was directly contested in real time by the podcast host, showing it is not an industry consensus even among AI-forward speakers.

Mike Charalambous (Co-CEO, Threedium), ep343: 'there's no longer a real relationship between a brand and a consumer. Brands think that they have a relationship with consumers, but the reality is that the relationship is with the likes of Amazon, Google, ChatGPT, Anthropic, TikTok, Instagram... agents even.' Host response, same episode: 'I also like the fact that you mentioned that there's no longer a real relationship between the brand and the consumer. I would... actually debate.'

## Absences (verified by exact match)

- The exact framing of the assignment's core question — whether observing the physical shopper becomes MORE valuable (last unmediated human signal) or LESS valuable (decision happened elsewhere) as buying moves to agents — was searched directly ('physical store shopper observation agentic AI', 'last unmediated human decision signal in store camera computer vision', 'does in-store data become more valuable when purchasing moves online or to AI') and never appears; no guest, operator or vendor, poses or answers this tradeoff.
- Exact-match count: 'agentic commerce' (the specific phrase, as opposed to the broader stemmed 'agentic') appears in only 18 chunks across 3 episodes (315, 300, 316-adjacent search results) — confirming the topic is concentrated in a handful of conversations, not spread evenly across the 21 'agentic' episodes.
- No guest quantifies actual agent-completed purchases today; the only concrete stat offered is NielsenIQ's Salim Bachatene citing 'around 30 percent... AI influenced' purchases — explicitly hedged as an industry estimate about influence, not completed agentic transactions, and not sourced to a specific study on air.
- No mention of the EU AI Act or any agentic-commerce-specific regulation in connection with this theme (consistent with the corpus-wide 'AI Act: 0' finding noted in the brief).
- No guest discusses Czech, Slovak, or other Central/Eastern European market specifics in relation to agentic commerce; the entire theme is carried by Western Europe, UK, US, and Gulf-region speakers.
- No operator or vendor in this cut discusses computer vision, demographic estimation, or in-store camera-based observation as a data asset relevant to an agentic-commerce future — the adjacent computer-vision material in the corpus (Ailet, Walkbase/STRATACACHE) is about retail-media measurement and loyalty attribution, not framed against agentic buying at all.

## Implications

- The addressable market is not evaporating on any horizon operators themselves believe in: two independent operators (Iceland's retail-media head, Danone's global sales director) put 60-90% of sales still happening physically today, with no near-term flip expected — a fair, defensible baseline for a CZ/SK sales narrative about the scale of the physical channel.
- The corpus gives zero borrowed authority for a 'physical shopper observation becomes MORE valuable because it's the last unmediated human signal' pitch — nobody in 232 episodes over 2.5 years frames it that way. This narrative would have to be invented and defended on our own evidence, not amplified as an existing industry view; leading a CZ/SK pitch with it risks sounding speculative to operators (Danone, Chalhoub) who are actively pushing back on agentic hype as premature for exactly this kind of futures-thinking.
- A genuine counter-risk: the category vendors flag as most exposed to near-term agentic disruption is routine/repeat FMCG grocery reordering — precisely the category base a CZ/SK in-store measurement business is likely to serve. If agent-driven reorder subscriptions grow (as Botify's CRO and Retail Economics' CEO argue), footfall and in-aisle decision-making for staples specifically could shrink even while total in-store revenue share stays high — the 'boring, high-frequency' purchases most amenable to passive observation may be exactly the ones that migrate away first, leaving mostly discovery/impulse/unplanned-purchase behavior to observe.
- The entire industry conversation captured here (retail media, GEO/AEO, PDP structured data, digital shelf) is fighting for mindshare and budget around winning machine legibility online — not physical behavior capture. If that framing dominates CZ/SK boardrooms too (plausible, since it's driven by the same global vendors — NielsenIQ, AWS-class cloud partners — active in the region), in-store shopper observation risks being positioned as a legacy/nice-to-have channel unless we actively construct the counter-narrative ourselves; there is no existing wave to ride.
- A usable, attributable talking point: StrataCache — running a business model structurally similar to ours (in-store screens plus shopper tracking) — is already telling its own retail-media customer base that in-store is comparatively insulated from AI/agentic disruption versus online. This can be cited as 'an in-store measurement vendor's own public position,' with appropriate caveats about self-interest, rather than as independent proof.
- Because Danone and Chalhoub — sophisticated, senior operators — explicitly characterize 'agentic' as premature hype relative to where 90% of current revenue and decision-making actually sits, a CZ/SK pitch grounded in today's proven in-store economics (incrementality, path-to-purchase, conversion) will likely land better with equally pragmatic regional buyers than one built primarily on speculative AI-displacement of the shopper.

# Computer vision, in-store AI, personalization, and the AI risk/regulation conversation. Corpus: "The FMCG Guys," 232 episodes, Mar 2024–Sep 2026, 166h, 14,940 chunks.

Computer vision in retail is a vendor category here, not an operator behavior: every "computer vision"/"image recognition" segment is spoken by someone selling it (Ailet for shelf-facing execution; STRATACACHE/Walkbase for shopper-facing sensing), and both shopper-facing vendors explicitly frame in-store cameras as a liability they engineer around, not a feature they sell — Walkbase's VP says "we tend to stay away from computer vision products" over biometric-capture risk, and STRATACACHE's CEO markets his product as working "without cameras, doing that fully GDPR or California Privacy approved." Personalization (111 chunks/43 eps, confirmed by exact match) is real and wanted, but when operators use the word they mean loyalty-tied offers or physical product customization (Bata's monogrammed shoes) — one industry commentator flatly says true in-store 1:1 personalization "right now... that's not realistic," proposing "relevant" as the honest substitute. The risk/regulation conversation is not silent as the brief's prior pass assumed: a fresh exact-match check overturns "hallucination: 0" (real count is 10 chunks/5 eps, including a substantive operator discussion) though it confirms "AI Act" is genuinely absent (all 3 raw hits are transcription noise for "AI actually," not the EU regulation). But the one deep risk conversation that exists — a single 68-minute Mars episode — is entirely about enterprise LLM/ChatGPT governance and never once touches cameras, biometrics, shopper consent, or demographic inference. Age/gender inference as a concept is entirely absent from the corpus. Net: the market appears to want personalization but not know it means observation, and nobody operator-side is yet naming the specific compliance question a computer-vision company sells the answer to — which is either a wide-open opportunity to educate a category before it forms an objection, or a sign we would be the ones introducing the word "camera" into a conversation currently going out of its way to avoid it.

## Implications

- The absence of camera-specific risk language is a genuine blind spot, not evidence the market is unbothered: the two vendors who do shopper-facing sensing (STRATACACHE/Walkbase) actively avoid cameras and market that avoidance as a GDPR/privacy selling point. That is a signal the market treats camera-based sensing as the harder sell, not an easier one — a compliance-first pitch for camera-based CV is not solving an imaginary problem, but it also is not walking into an active, articulated objection; it is introducing a word ("camera") that competitors have deliberately kept out of the conversation.
- Because operators define "personalization" as loyalty-tied offers or physical customization, not as attribute inference, a pitch that leads with "we personalize based on what the camera sees" risks landing as a category operators do not yet have a mental slot for. The stronger entry point suggested by the evidence is measurement/execution framing (what Ailet and Carlsberg's rep use case demonstrate operators already value: image recognition for KPIs, not for identity) rather than a shopper-personalization framing.
- The one deep operator conversation about AI risk that exists (Mars) is entirely about enterprise LLM governance (ChatGPT/Claude data leakage, hallucination, agentic autonomy) and never touches physical/in-store AI. This means brand-side risk officers who ARE thinking hard about AI governance currently have no vocabulary or reference point for in-store computer vision risk specifically — a compliance-first pitch to this audience may need to build the frame from scratch rather than plug into an existing concern, which is a heavier lift than the brief assumed but also means no incumbent objection to overcome.
- No operator anywhere in 166 hours discusses age/gender inference, k-anonymity, or receipt-matching as a category — meaning there is no evidence of buyer-side awareness of exactly the aggregation/anonymization safeguards (k>=25, no identification) that differentiate this product's risk posture. That gap cuts both ways for a Czechia/Slovakia pitch: it is likely unclaimed territory to define first, but it also means a retailer or brand procurement/legal team encountering this pitch for the first time will have to be walked through why it is different from the camera-based tracking two vendors in this same corpus explicitly avoid — the pitch needs to preemptively distinguish itself from "computer vision products" as Walkbase uses the term, not assume the distinction is already understood.
- GDPR fluency already exists among in-store vendors and is used as a competitive claim (STRATACACHE, Walkbase both cite GDPR/California Privacy compliance as a feature). This validates that compliance messaging resonates as a sales lever in this specific vendor category — but it also means the market has already normalized "we comply with GDPR" as table stakes, so a CZ/SK pitch leaning only on GDPR compliance will read as parity, not differentiation; the k>=25 aggregation and no-identification design need to be argued as going meaningfully beyond what STRATACACHE/Walkbase already claim, not merely restating GDPR adequacy.
- The complete silence on "AI Act" as a substantive regulatory topic (as opposed to a transcription artifact) suggests EU AI Act biometric-categorization risk tiers are not yet on FMCG/retail executives' radar in this corpus's timeframe (through Sep 2026) — which argues for treating AI Act compliance as an educational opening (a chance to be the one who names the specific regulatory exposure of camera-based shopper inference) rather than assuming buyers already feel that pressure and are shopping for a fix.

# Deployed vs. hyped AI in FMCG, Mar 2024–Sep 2026 (operator evidence only ranked; vendor context flagged)

Real, working AI in FMCG today is narrower and duller than the marketing suggests: content generation (product descriptions, translation, tagging), demand forecasting/marketing-mix modeling, and internal productivity copilots are the three use cases operators actually name with numbers — and even those come with explicit caveats (partial accuracy, human-in-the-loop, hallucination management). The single most consistent operator message across the whole window is "pilot purgatory": near-universal experimentation, thin scaling — Mars puts it at 88% of companies with AI in at least one function but only ~35% past minimal use, and under 30% of leaders confident their pilots are scalable. The blocker operators name is not the model but data governance and organizational alignment (Henkel, The Very Group, Miroglio all describe governance failures as their biggest AI lesson). "Responsible AI" as a named discipline is a distinctly late-corpus phenomenon (Mars, May 2026: barely existed 18 months earlier). The tested prior finding — AI "became fashionable a year ago, we're not hearing much about it now" — traces to one March 2024 comment from a retail-media exec, and the corpus's own volume trend runs the opposite direction: AI-mention prevalence per episode rose from roughly 40-47% of episodes in 2024 to over 70% by mid-2026, so that finding is not borne out as a durable trend. Most relevant to our own space: two in-store measurement vendors in the exact competitive niche (STRATACACHE and its Walkbase unit) explicitly design around cameras and computer vision, citing GDPR and retailer nervousness about biometric data — no operator anywhere in the corpus describes having deployed a named, working camera-based shopper-demographic system in a physical store.

## Findings

### [operator / high] Content generation at the SKU/asset level (product descriptions, translation, image tagging) is the clearest genuinely-deployed, measured AI win, with real before/after numbers from named operators.

The Very Group (Michelle Williams, Data Product Owner): generative product-description tool went from an 8-week POC (Jan 2024) to live production (May 2024) and is now running across ~40,000 fashion SKUs, cutting write time 'down from 30 minutes down to two minutes,' still with a human-in-the-loop review UI. Miroglio Group (Matteo Loro/Marcello Offi): AI tagging/description pipeline achieved '1 minute and 30 seconds per product versus five description per hour done by humans,' translation into 6 languages '8 time faster,' and 'translation cost per single product reduced by 80%.'

### [operator / high] AI-driven demand forecasting and marketing-mix modeling are real production systems at scale operators, but explicitly imperfect and only partially deployed across the portfolio — and operators contrast their own honesty about this with vendors who oversell coverage.

Pierre-Yves Calloc'h, CDO at Pernod Ricard (Oct 2024): 'we can make... marketing mix modeling only on eight to 10 brands with a reasonable accuracy... it's working on those eight brands but on those other seven it doesn't work,' contrasted with an external vendor who would 'give you a prediction on 15 brands because you've paid for it.'

### [operator / high] The dominant operator self-assessment across the corpus is 'pilot purgatory' — broad experimentation, thin scaling to real business impact — not stalled-and-abandoned, but stuck below maturity.

Patricia Amaro, Global Digital Commerce Sr Director at Mars (May 2026): '88% of companies declare that they have at least one business function with AI. But I think it's 35%... that is like really getting to it... it's almost everything instill in pilot mode, still... waiting to see the capture'; and 'less than 30% of leaders of CEOs are confident that what they are doing in a pilot is possible... scalable in the short term.'

### [operator / high] The blocker operators name most is not the AI model itself but data governance and cross-functional alignment — and multiple operators describe this as their single biggest AI-related failure.

Boris Rütten, Global Head of eCommerce at Henkel: 'the governance from my perspective is the one which is most underestimated and one of the biggest pitfalls... we kept adding data sources but we didn't take care of the governance enough... you lose the credibility, you lose the buy in of the entire organization and then it fails.' ## Implications

- The macro tailwind is real, not fading: episode-level AI-mention prevalence rose from ~40-47% (2024) to over 70% (2026), and 'governance'/'responsible AI' as topics are also rising, not declining — the tested hypothesis that interest peaked and faded is not supported. This favors leading with an AI/data-capture pitch now rather than waiting out a hype cycle.
- But the operator-proven use cases (content generation, forecasting/MMM, internal copilots) are all things brands do to their own first-party data and assets — none involve buying third-party physical-world observational data. We have essentially zero named-operator evidence that FMCG brands or retailers have already validated and scaled a camera-based in-store shopper-observation product; treat this as a first-mover, not an 'everyone's already doing this' pitch, and expect a genuinely unproven-category sales conversation rather than a competitive-parity one.
- Argues against our position: the two vendors most directly adjacent to our space (STRATACACHE, Walkbase) have deliberately built their entire in-store measurement stack to avoid cameras and computer vision, specifically because retailers are 'conservative... when it comes to privacy' about biometric-adjacent data and because GDPR compliance is 'quite easy to do' without cameras. If sophisticated, well-funded competitors in this exact niche have concluded cameras are more liability than they're worth, a shopper-observation product built on cameras/computer vision should expect this to be the first objection from EU retailers and DPOs — not a hypothetical concern but the industry's own considered choice.
- The strongest available counter to that objection is the operators' own repeated framing: what they say they need is not less AI but more governance — provenance, human-in-the-loop review options, explainable accuracy numbers, and an opt-in/consent story analogous to loyalty-program frameworks. A product pitched primarily on data-governance rigor and a documented DPIA/anonymization methodology (rather than on AI sophistication) will land closer to what Henkel, The Very Group, and Mars all independently describe as their actual blocker.
- Sales cycles should assume 'pilot purgatory' as the default buyer state (Mars: under 30% of leaders trust their own pilots are scalable) and design a fast, narrow, numbers-first proof motion — the operator wins we found (Very Group, Miroglio) all cite a short, bounded POC (8-12 weeks) that produced a hard before/after metric before any scale conversation. Vendor pitches in this corpus that lead with big narrative claims (PunttAI) read as less credible to sophisticated buyers than operators' own modest, partial, caveat-laden numbers — our materials should mirror the latter register, not the former.
- Given operators describe still fighting to govern and trust their own core commerce/loyalty/POS data (Henkel's reversed-pyramid lesson, Very Group's AI Guild), a third-party observational data layer may be judged as premature by brands/retailers that haven't yet built basic data governance — a real risk that our sales motion may need to include helping the buyer's own data governance story, not just supplying a new signal on top of an ungoverned foundation.
