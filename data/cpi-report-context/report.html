Daily pattern. Footfall builds through the morning, peaks in the early-to-mid afternoon, and tapers into the evening. Very few visitors are recorded before 7:00 or after 21:00.

Weekly pattern. Saturday is consistently the busiest day of the week; Thursday is consistently the quietest. On a per-day basis, weekends draw roughly 12% more visitors than an average weekday.

Who shows up when. Different visitor types don't just add up to the daily curve above — each has its own rhythm inside it. Retirees peak mid-morning and fade through the day; parents hold a steady presence through the whole shopping day rather than a single peak; students build through the afternoon into an after-school peak; young adults become steadily more dominant into the evening.

Solo vs. accompanied traffic follows the same daily curve in reverse. Visitors are most likely to be alone right at opening and closing. On weekends, they're least likely to be alone right around midday (as low as 60% alone at noon); on weekdays the lowest point comes later, around 18:00 (66% alone). Weekend visitors are more likely to be accompanied than weekday visitors at every single hour of the day — this isn't just a byproduct of weekends being busier, it holds hour-for-hour.

Child-accompanied traffic is a weekend story, and an all-day story. Visits involving a child — whether with one parent or the whole family — are more common on weekends than weekdays (10.5% of all weekend visits vs. 7.6% of weekday visits). On weekdays specifically, this traffic doesn't cluster around school drop-off/pickup times — it holds a steady, flat share from mid-morning to early evening, more consistent with parents of pre-school-age children than a school-run errand.

What this suggests for operations: staffing, on-floor promotions, and event scheduling deliver the most impact concentrated in the 11:00–16:00 window, with extra weight on Saturday. Morning hours (8:00–10:00) are the natural window for anything aimed at older shoppers, before the crowd — and family-facing amenities (seating, changing facilities, stroller access) earn their keep across the whole week, not just weekends, since parent traffic doesn't disappear on weekdays. Weekend programming should assume a more social, more accompanied audience even during the same hours a weekday sees mostly solo visitors. And any weekend-specific activation should be targeted, not blanket — Active Adults in Athleisure and Teenagers in Youth Culture are the personas actually driving the weekend uplift (+43% and +35%), while Retirees and Polished Professionals are, if anything, more of a weekday audience.

Age skews young-to-middle, not old. Visitors in their 20s-40s make up two-thirds of all traffic; visitors 60+ are the smallest bracket after teens.

% of all visitors in each age bracket, as read from the photo (not a self-reported age).

By life stage, established adults and young adults are almost the whole picture. Together they're nearly three-quarters of all traffic; parents and students are real but much smaller pools.

% of all visitors reading as each life-stage category.

Appearance tells one consistent story: mainstream, not styled-up. Build reads as "average" for 81% of visitors, athletic for 12%, and everything else (soft, lean, unclear, plus-size) in the low single digits each. Grooming reads "standard" (not high-effort) for 95%. Makeup reads "none" for the largest single group of women at every age bracket, once you set aside cases where it simply couldn't be judged — it's never "subtle" or "prominent" instead. This isn't one field's quirk — it's three independent visual reads (build, grooming, makeup) all landing on the same "unremarkable, low-effort" picture of the population, on top of the same conclusion Section 1's persona lens already reached from the timing data.

% of all visitors by build/physique archetype.

Two smaller appearance facts, not charted: visible tattoos are more common on men than women (11% vs 7%, of those where skin was visible enough to judge). Skin tone reads light for 71% of visitors and medium for 29% — expected for this market, included here as a population fact rather than a finding.

Most visitors come alone; of those who don't, it's mostly mixed-gender. 70% of all visits are solo. Of the 30% that involve a companion, six in ten are mixed-gender groups — same-gender groups (all-female or all-male) are each smaller, and all-female groups outnumber all-male ones.

% of grouped (non-solo) visits by the group's gender composition — e.g. "60%" mixed-gender means 60% of the 30% of visits that involve a companion, not 60% of all visits.

What this suggests for operations: the demographic base is broad (young-to-middle-aged, mainstream-presenting) rather than niche, which supports general-audience tenant mix and marketing over narrow targeting. Where targeting does make sense, the personas give a sharper handle than raw demographics alone — e.g. reaching "parents" as a group is really two different asks (established-adult parents vs. the Parents with a Natural Look persona, itself nearly all-female and 30s-skewed), and the retiree audience is essentially one clean, well-defined persona rather than a fuzzy age cutoff. And since most non-solo visits are mixed-gender rather than same-gender groups, promotions framed around couples, partners, or families reach a bigger share of the accompanied audience than "bring your girlfriends" or "lads' day out"-style same-gender framing would.

Spending signal. Most visitors read as mid-market spenders. A meaningful minority — 6.4% of all visitors — carry at least one item that reads as premium or luxury tier: a small but clearly identifiable higher-spend segment, not noise. A further one in five outfits simply don't give enough visible signal to place on the spend scale at all, and are reported that way rather than defaulted into "mid-market."

% of all visitors, whole-outfit spend tier. "Unclear" is a genuine answer, not missing data — it means there wasn't enough visible signal to judge, not that judgment was skipped.

Branding is the exception, not the rule. No identifiable brand-tier is readable on 64% of outfits, and 76% show no visible logo at all. Individual items carry a brand ID only 9% of the time — but since people wear multiple items at once, that still adds up to 32% of visitors having at least one branded item somewhere on them. Two things sharpen this picture: a premium or luxury read is essentially always tied to a specific identified brand — among items with no brand identified, essentially none were still read as premium or luxury — so "premium" is best understood as a brand-recognition signal, not an independent quality judgment. And of the items where a brand WAS identified, roughly half came from recognizing a design or silhouette (5.4% of all items) rather than a readable logo (5.7% combined) — the adidas stripes or a Birkenstock sole shape count as much as an actual wordmark.

Accessories and carried items are a smaller, different story. Only half of all visitors (50%) show any visible accessory at all, and just over a third (38%) are carrying anything. Watches lead accessories; phones and shopping trolleys lead carried items. One honest wrinkle: bags don't sort into one clean bucket — a crossbody bag shows up as a garment for 9.8% of all visitors, as an accessory for 4.5%, and as a carried item for 3.0%, depending on how it's worn or held in each specific case. That's a real, case-by-case read, not an inconsistency smoothed over for tidiness.

Through the lens of visitor personas. Spend and brand signals split the 8 personas sharply — and loud branding and high spend signal turn out not to be the same thing at all.

Two personas make the clearest contrast. Polished Professionals in Smart Casual lead premium/luxury spend signal by a wide margin, yet sit near the bottom on loud branding — a classic quiet-luxury pattern. Teenagers in Youth Culture sit at the opposite end: middling spend signal, but the second-loudest, most logo-forward presentation of any persona — loud branding here doesn't track with actually spending more. Active Adults in Athleisure is a third, different pattern worth naming on its own: both high spend signal AND the loudest branding of any persona — logo-forward premium sportswear, where loud and high-spend genuinely go together rather than pulling apart.

What this suggests for operations: the visitor base reads as mainstream and largely unbranded, which argues for a value-forward, general-audience tenant and merchandising mix over a premium-first strategy — the ~6% carrying a premium/luxury-tier item are a real but narrow segment better served by targeted outreach than a broad repositioning. Because only half of visitors show any accessory and barely a third carry anything, accessory- and carry-forward categories (jewellery, eyewear, tech accessories, bags) are working from a smaller addressable share of foot traffic than apparel is. The small but real non-shopper long tail — tradespeople, delivery, security — is worth a line in any footfall-based planning as genuine traffic, not noise to filter out. And premium messaging aimed at Polished Professionals should lean on quality and fit rather than logo visibility — that segment's own spend signal is real but quiet; loud branding on its own (as with Teenagers in Youth Culture) isn't a reliable stand-in for higher spend, though for Active Adults in Athleisure it genuinely is both.
