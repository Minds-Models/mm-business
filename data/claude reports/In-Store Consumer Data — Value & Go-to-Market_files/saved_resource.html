01 · The asset

What you can actually sell today — and what you can't

The product is a visit-level census of physical shoppers: ~88 visual attributes per person (demographics, apparel brands worn, spending tier, life stage, smoking type…) matched to receipts at 92–93 % via time correlation. No cross-visit ID (and none possible in the EU) — so you sell who buys what, where, when, not shopper frequency or household penetration. That makes you a complement to NielsenIQ's "what sold" data, not a replacement — and a GDPR-native one, which in Europe is a moat, not a handicap.

02 · The benchmark

The NielsenIQ spread is the whole business model

Your three internal data points, side by side, show where the margin lives in retail data — annual CZK flows around NielsenIQ in the Czech market:

DM receives · raw POS, 261 drugstores 0,9 M CZK/yr (≈ 287 CZK/store/mo)
PMI pays · tobacco category read, petrol + convenience (2021) 8,0 M CZK/yr
Nestlé pays · broad food-retail coverage (2023) 18,5 M CZK/yr
Gray = supply side (what a retailer is paid for raw data). Blue = demand side (what one brand pays for the derived, syndicated product). Same underlying asset — roughly a 20–60× markup, multiplied again because the identical category read is sold to every brand in the category. Note DM's raw-feed price may be barter-adjusted (data-for-benchmarks), so the true supply-side cash price is even lower.

Three conclusions fall straight out of this:

  • Never be the DM of this equation. Selling raw or lightly-processed feeds to an aggregator prices you at ~300 CZK/store/month. Your entire cost base (<1 000 CZK/store/mo) only works if you sell the derived, syndicated product yourself.
  • Syndication is the multiplier. NIQ sells the same tobacco read to PMI and JTI and BAT. One dataset, N invoices. This is why you must never grant a brand category exclusivity — it would cap the model at 1× instead of 3–4×.
  • The barter mechanic is your rights weapon. Retailers already accept "data in exchange for analytics and benchmarks" from NIQ. Offer the same trade — free insight dashboard + revenue share in exchange for a monetization license — and you're asking for something the market has already normalized, while paying better than NIQ does.
03 · Willingness to pay

Who pays what, at which density

The critical distinction: below ~30 stores you sell insight (bought on interestingness, from innovation/insight budgets — density is irrelevant, credibility is the gate). Above it you sell measurement (bought on representativeness, from recurring data budgets — this is where NIQ-benchmarked money appears). Nobody needs a full census; NIQ itself extrapolates from samples. The gate is buyer perception of coverage, and NIQ has already trained the market on what "enough" looks like.

External validation (19 Jul research pass): a real Czech buyer — the state agricultural institute ÚZEI — pays ACNielsen Czech Republic 3,7 M CZK/yr for a broad monthly retail sell-out feed (public contract registry, Nov 2022) and paid only ~230–240 k CZK for narrow single-category reports — a 10–15× spread that independently mirrors this ladder's study-vs-subscription pricing. US comparables bracket the same band: ongoing syndicated subscriptions run $100 k–999 k/yr (≈ 2–20 M CZK), Circana MULO contracts $60–150 k+, SPINS $30–100 k. Most importantly, US household-panel evidence shows "who-buys" data prices at or above "what-sold" POS data despite weaker samples — so the 1–8 M band is defensible today and, for the who-layer at maturity, likely conservative.

Use the tobacco meeting to calibrate this table, not to guess. The single most valuable question you can ask three tobacco majors in one room is: "At what coverage would this augment or replace your NIQ line — chain-level, or full channel?" Their answer is the density requirement. Let the buyers price the density gate for you.

07 · Rights architecture

The retailer deal: pay better than NIQ, take the license

One standard Data Partner Agreement, offered to every retailer, structured as a trade the market already understands:

Retailer gets
  • Free insight dashboard — personas, traffic, conversion, category reads for their own stores (your marginal cost ≈ 0).
  • A majority revenue share, tiered by product — ~40–50 % of data-product revenue and ~60–70 % of retail-media revenue on their audience, vs NIQ's ~300 CZK/store/mo flat. Why the majority, not the old 25–30 %: it's the arithmetic that clears a giant retailer's 10 M threshold (§8), not generosity.
  • A hard data firewall — brands only ever see aggregated shopper audience data; the retailer's margins, sell-through and price data (their buying leverage) never leave the building.
  • Attribution protection — chain identity masked in channel aggregates by default; named only if they opt in; their data never shown chain-attributed to a named competitor.
  • Category retail-media priority — first right to the RMN products built on their stores (SuperZoo's stated interest).
M&M gets — non-negotiable
  • Perpetual, irrevocable license to sell derived, aggregated, k-anonymized (k ≥ 25) insights to third parties.
  • POS feed for all instrumented stores, receipt-level.
  • Direct contract — at EuroOil, data rights sit M&M ↔ EuroOil; Moris stays on media/fit-out only.
  • No brand-side category exclusivity, ever — syndication is the business. Concede at most a 3-month early-access window on custom cuts.
  • M&M is exclusive analytics provider on instrumented stores for the term.

Sequencing across the four retailers

  1. SuperZoo first, this month. Warmest relationship, real POS, marketing advocate, retail-media pull. Bundle the rights clause into the 10–15 (push 30) store expansion contract. This becomes your template and your proof that a retailer will sign it.
  2. EuroOil second, via brand demand. Don't ask for rights in the abstract — walk in after the tobacco meeting with "brands are ready to pay X for the linked read; it requires your POS feed and a data agreement; your share is Y." Disclose the data play before the meeting (see §9). New intel from the public contract registry: ČEPRO — EuroOil's state-owned parent — expanded its ACNielsen contract in June 2025 to include TSR data. NIQ is already selling into your retailer's parent: the data budget and data literacy exist on their side, and the clock on NIQ deepening that relationship is running. Move first. (Single-source, hlidacstatu.cz — verify the scope before citing it in negotiation.)
  3. GymBeam: keep as lab. No brand-resale value, but it's your methodology showcase and where you harden match-rate + suppression standards. Ask for rights anyway (cheap to grant, keeps the template consistent).
  4. Teta > O2 in the pipeline. Teta is a drugstore chain of 511 with a competitor (DM) already monetized by NIQ — the sharpest pitch you have. O2 adds little to the data business; serve it with the SaaS, don't spend rights-negotiation energy there.
08 · Retailer economics

The 10 M threshold — why the split is arithmetic, not generosity

A 15-year brand insider's rule of thumb: a tier-1 grocer (Billa-scale) won't seriously engage unless there's ≥ 10 M CZK/yr of upside for them, and those calls happen at CEO level over a ~2-year cycle. That single number, combined with the rev-share split, quietly decides whether the biggest retailers are reachable at all — because it dictates how many brands must buy on a retailer's stores to clear the bar.

How many brands per retailer to clear 10 M CZK for the retailer

Price per brand / yrOld grab — retailer 28 %Balanced — 50 %Win-win — 65 %
Chain-category sub · ~2 M~18 brands~10 brands~8 brands
Channel currency · ~5 M~7 brands~4 brands~3 brands
Channel + RMN media · ~8 M~4–5 brands~2–3 brands~2 brands

This is the punchline the earlier draft missed: at the old 25–30 % grab, a giant needs 11–18 brands buying on its stores to care — commercially near-impossible to assemble. Give the retailer the majority, and the bar drops to 3–8 brands — reachable, especially in grocery where dozens of FMCG brands exist. The win-win split isn't a concession; it's the arithmetic that makes the biggest retailers — where the 100–200 M cap lives (§5) — addressable at all.

So does it change the value of the model? Yes — two-sidedly

Net: lower revenue per deal, higher expected value. You trade share-of-each-euro for (1) a much larger, actually-closeable market — grocery giants plus the conflict-blocked deals the old grab would have killed — and (2) the multiple: securing the perpetual aggregate-rights license (§7) turns you from a ~4–8× enablement vendor into a ~8–15× measurement currency. The revenue line goes down; the enterprise value goes up, because closeability × addressable market × multiple beats the per-deal haircut. Full logic in the win-win & valuation companion.

Two pools, two splits

  • Data / measurement product (the syndicated reads priced in §3): retailer ~40–50 %, M&M keeps the majority. Still a large step up from NIQ's ~300 CZK/store/mo, and enough to move a mid-size retailer.
  • Retail-media revenue (brands paying to reach the retailer's audience on-screen): retailer keeps ~60–70 %, the RMN norm; M&M takes an infra/measurement cut. This is the bigger pool and the one that clears the 10 M giant bar.
  • The one non-negotiable across both: the perpetual, irrevocable aggregate license (§7). Concede share; never concede the cross-retailer aggregate — that clause is the difference between the enablement multiple and the currency multiple.
Implication for the pipeline. The 10 M bar is a giant-retailer gate (Billa/COOP/Albert), not a near-term one — SuperZoo, EuroOil and Teta are smaller, with a lower threshold to engage. And the real constraint on landing a giant isn't finding brands (grocers have dozens) — it's assembling enough brand demand first, which is exactly what the tobacco meeting and brand LOIs (§9) are for. You cannot walk into Billa until you can credibly promise them their 10 M.
09 · The meeting · JTI + PMI + BAT + Asahi

Playbook: fund the network, price the data, sell nothing yet

The meeting's stated agenda is personalization and who pays the €120/store. Play that agenda — and use the room for the data business's price discovery. You cannot sell data (no rights); you can collect commitments contingent on rights.

Walk out with (in priority order)

  1. Brand co-funding of the 50-store EuroOil wave. 50 stores × €120 × 12 = €72 k/yr total; split 3–4 ways it's €18–24 k per brand — a rounding error next to PMI's ≈ €326 k NIQ line. Frame it as share-of-screen plus data readiness: the brands are financing the sensor network that will produce the category read.
  2. Per-brand LOI for a paid category pilot — 250–500 k CZK, on the 50-store wave, explicitly "subject to retailer data agreement." Non-binding is fine; the LOIs are your leverage instrument with EuroOil.
  3. The density answer — "at what coverage does this augment or replace your NIQ line?" Get each brand's number on record.
  4. The budget owner — named contact in insights/trade marketing per brand, and how they buy (annual syndication vs projects).

Discovery questions that price the product for you

  • What do you buy today for this channel (NIQ, agencies), and what does it not tell you?
  • What would monthly switching-to-smokeless by shopper persona and daypart, at the shelf be worth — and which budget buys it?
  • For Asahi separately: same structure for beer/NAB in convenience — and gauge appetite for a COOP-scale channel read later. Asahi is your seed for the FMCG vertical, not a tobacco afterthought.

Landmines

The illustrative deck. The EuroOil×JTI POS layer is modeled. Label every such slide "modeled illustration" and say it out loud once. Tobacco majors are compliance-heavy; one probing question that exposes fabricated-looking numbers ends the relationship. Show GymBeam (real, p-values, honest nulls) as the methodology proof instead.
EuroOil hears about it secondhand. You're meeting their category's brands while holding no data rights from them. Call EuroOil before the meeting: "we'll also probe brands' willingness to pay for linked insights — if it lands, you get POS-gated revenue share." Turns a betrayal narrative into an alignment one, and it's consistent with the disclosed-anchor-tenant strategy you already chose.
Exclusivity trap. If a brand offers to fund more in exchange for exclusive data access — decline. Category exclusivity kills the syndication multiplier (§2). Offer the early-access window instead. Explicitly normalize it: "this works like NIQ — every brand in the category buys the same currency."
Age language. Never "age verification" — always "age estimation with error bands." An over-claim in a tobacco context becomes discoverable evidence against the brand, and their lawyers know it.
Price anchoring. Keep the €120 personalization fee and data pricing on separate paper, always. The device fee is infrastructure; data products price against NIQ lines, not against hardware.
