The retailer win-win — and the GTM reality behind it

How to sell in-store shopper intelligence without it looking like we're arming the retailer's counterparty. Built on a 15-year brand insider's read of how retailers and brands actually behave — which forced a real correction to the earlier plan.

Companion to the strategy report Source insider · 15+ yrs at brands, 10 at P&G Date 22 Jul 2026
The turn in one paragraph

The retailer will never let us sell their shopper data to brands for a cut — because that data is their leverage in the negotiation they care about most: buying from those same brands. So the model can't be "we monetize your data." It has to be "we build you your own dunnhumby": the retailer monetizes their own audience to brands, keeps control and the majority of the money, and we're the intelligence layer underneath. Two guardrails make it safe — a hard firewall between trading data (never leaves the retailer) and audience/media data (the only thing brands ever see), and a value proposition where our data strengthens the retailer's hand against brands instead of weakening it.

01 · The objection, stated plainly

Why the obvious model looks like theft

The earlier plan — sell shopper insights to brands, give the retailer a rev-share — assumed the retailer is a willing data supplier. The insider says that assumption is exactly backwards:

"Data is extremely valuable to retailers. They absolutely do not want to share any information with the brands under any circumstances — it would weaken their negotiating position during purchasing. Finding a win-win with retailers is the biggest challenge." — brand insider, 15+ years

The logic is airtight from the retailer's chair: the retailer's profit is made buying well from brands. Shopper insight — who buys, how much, how price-sensitive — is the leverage in that negotiation. Hand it to the brand and you've sold the retailer's poker hand to the player across the table. That's why our model "seems like stealing" — from the retailer's side, it is. Any GTM that doesn't resolve this dies at the first serious retailer conversation, and those conversations happen at CEO level over a ~2-year cycle, so we don't get many shots.

02 · The unlock

There are two datasets, and retailers feel opposite things about them

"Shopper data" is not one thing. Inside it are two datasets a retailer treats completely differently — and our visual data sits entirely in the safe one.

Never leaves the retailer
Trading data — their leverage
  • Sell-through by SKU, velocity, share
  • Margins & cost, price elasticity
  • Promo response, listing performance
  • Anything that reveals what the retailer will pay or accept from a brand
Monetizable to brands
Audience / media data — our layer
  • Who's in the store — demographics, personas, life stage
  • Engagement & dwell, who saw an ad and responded
  • Aggregated, anonymized audience segments for targeting & measurement
  • The "who" a retail media network sells — and cameras see it, POS panels don't

This is the whole unlock. Selling audience data for media does not weaken a retailer's purchasing position — it's a separate value pool, which is exactly why every major grocer already sells it (below). Selling trading data would be suicide, and we simply never do it. Say this out loud, first, in every retailer conversation: "your margins, your sell-through, your negotiating data never leave this building — we deal only in aggregated shopper audiences, and you decide what's exposed."

03 · The model

Retailer as monetizer — not source

The role change that resolves everything: the retailer doesn't hand us data to sell. The retailer monetizes their own audience to brands, on their terms, and we're the infrastructure that makes their audience uniquely valuable and measurable. 04 · The value proposition to the retailer

Three things they get — and none of it costs them leverage

Pillar 1
A new profit center

A retail-media / audience-licensing revenue stream monetizing their own shoppers — high margin, built on our infra, no capex, no data-science team to hire. They keep the majority.

Pillar 2
Total control & a firewall

They decide what's packaged and exposed. Trading data never leaves. Aggregated, anonymized, k-anonymous audiences only. Their leverage is contractually and technically protected.

Pillar 3
A sharper hand vs brands

The same who + conversion data makes them a smarter category manager and tougher negotiator. Our data strengthens their buying position — the opposite of the fear.

What you actually say to the retailer CEO
"We turn your stores into a measurable, monetizable audience — the way Tesco and Kroger did — but you keep total control and the majority of the new revenue. Your margins, costs and sell-through never leave the building. Brands only ever see aggregated shopper audiences, for media. And the same intelligence that earns you that new revenue also makes you a smarter buyer against those brands. We're not asking you to pay us or share your leverage — we're building you a new profit center and taking a cut of what the brands pay."

Note what this pitch is not: it is not "personalization" (the insider is blunt that retailers don't care what sells, so they won't buy it), and it is not "give us your data." It's "we build and run your audience-monetization business."

05 · GTM reality

Long cycles at the top, fast wins in the middle

"A large retailer like Billa won't even talk unless there's at least 10M CZK of upside for them. Decisions are made at CEO level. Our sales cycle with large retailers averaged two years — even with contacts." — brand insider
  • Reframe the 10M ask: never ask a giant to pay 10M — offer to create 10M+. A retail-media network on their audience is a new profit center, not a cost line. The conversation becomes "here's a new revenue stream," not "here's an invoice."
  • Don't lead GTM with grocery giants. Billa/COOP/Albert are 2-year, CEO-gated, ~2028–29 outcomes. Start those cycles now, in parallel, but don't stake near-term revenue on them.
  • Lead with mid-size & specialist — which is exactly your live pipeline. EuroOil, SuperZoo, Teta, GymBeam sit below Billa-tier: shorter cycles, less bureaucracy, decisions in months. Prove the model and generate the case studies there.
  • Use brand pull to compress the giant cycle. Brands "pay for almost anything" (below). If a tobacco major or a P&G-type tells a retailer "we want in-store measurement on your stores," you arrive invited, not cold — the single fastest way to shorten a 2-year cycle. The tobacco meeting can plant exactly this.
06 · The brand side — a correction

Sell measurement, not personas

"At P&G we already had very well-developed buyer personas — I wouldn't see that much value in visual shopper data. On the other hand, P&G has enormous money and was willing to pay for almost anything — hundreds of millions of CZK on market research." — brand insider, 10 yrs at P&G

This is a useful corrective that changes the brand pitch. To a sophisticated brand, "here are demographic personas" is commoditized — they have better ones. The value we sell them is not profiling; it's what even P&G can't buy:

  • Observed, in-store, closed-loop measurement — who actually engaged at the shelf and bought, attributable to a campaign, in real time. Personas are profiling (they have it); this is measurement (they don't).
  • The budget is bottomless — you just have to be in the right line item. "Pays for almost anything" is the real signal: sell into the measurement / currency budget, not the research / panel budget.

Two things keep the persona value alive, so we don't over-correct: (1) most brands aren't P&G — your actual pipeline (tobacco, pet, beer, mid-tier) has nothing like P&G's persona depth and will value the raw insight; (2) even for the giants, persona discovery being commoditized doesn't make in-store measurement commoditized — that gap is real, and it's what their bottomless research budget exists to fill. Segment the brand pitch: giants buy currency & measurement; mid-tier buys insight & personas.

07 · What this changes vs the earlier plan

Three corrections, honestly logged

Was
M&M sells shopper data to brands; retailer takes a ~28% rev-share as supplier.
Now
Retailer monetizes their own audience via our infra and keeps the majority; M&M takes an infra/measurement margin. The 28% split was likely backwards — this lowers our per-euro take but makes the deal closeable.
Was (a brief detour)
Charge the retailer for the insight dashboard — "they pay millions for data anyway."
Now — reverted
That rested on a misread (the "500k–1M CZK/month" figure was the insider's own product, not retailer data spend). Dashboard goes back to cheap/free barter in exchange for data rights — as the original report proposed.
Was
Lead brand pitch with persona / demographic depth.
Now
Lead with in-store closed-loop measurement; keep personas for mid-tier brands only. Segment the pitch by brand sophistication.

The net effect is a cleaner, more honest model. It removes a tempting shortcut ("retailers are big data buyers, just sell to them") we had no evidence for, and it replaces "monetize their data" (theft) and "sell them dashboards" (unproven wallet) with a single defensible motion: the retailer is a partner you win with control, rights and operational upside — not a customer you invoice — and the money comes from brands through a channel the retailer owns.

Open question to validate — don't build on it yet. We now have no reliable read on whether retailers will pay directly for operational intelligence. If they will, "ops intelligence sold to the retailer" is a real second revenue line; if they won't, it's purely the barter that earns the rights. Get the insider — or a retailer contact — to say what a Czech retailer's actual annual external data/analytics spend looks like before we model any retailer-direct revenue.

08 · Valuation lens

Closeable is not the same as valuable

Retailer-as-monetizer maximizes closeability — it gets you in the door and defuses the conflict. It does not, on its own, maximize valuation. Those are different objectives, and confusing them is where companies in this space leave most of their value behind. Valuation is set by one question: what do you own that no one can take back or rebuild? That sorts the three possible structures.

StructureWhat you ownWho owns the assetMultiple & ceiling
Pure enablement
retailer-as-monetizer, nothing more		 The tooling Retailer owns data, audience & brand relationship ~4–8× · capped; disintermediable the day they build in-house (Cooler Screens fate). You built dunnhumby — but it's Tesco's.
Independent currency
own the cross-retailer aggregate		 The dataset, the rights, the brand relationships You ~8–15× · a cross-retailer moat no single retailer can replicate. This is why NIQ is a $4B company.
Hybrid — enablement surface, owned aggregate core Perpetual aggregate license + infra Retailer owns their slice; you own the blend Highest risk-adjusted path to the high multiple. The wedge funds deployment; the aggregate is the value.

The valuation lives in the second row; retailer-as-monetizer alone is the first. So on its own it caps your ceiling. The resolution is the hybrid: concede the per-retailer economics (they keep the majority, control their taps) precisely because the per-retailer cut isn't where your value is — the value is the aggregate, which exposes no single retailer's data and is a genuinely different product. Being generous per-retailer and owning the aggregate are fully compatible.

The one term that decides your valuation

Not the rev-share percentage. It's whether you retain a perpetual, irrevocable license to the de-identified, aggregated, cross-retailer layer (held in a separate entity). That single clause is the difference between a ~5× enablement vendor and a ~12× measurement currency.

Give ground on almost everything a retailer can see and touch — the RMN split, their control, the free dashboard. Hold the line on that one clause. Fight for it explicitly, disclose it (hiding it is the landmine), and trade away nearly anything else to keep it.

The acquirer test — the cleanest way to see it

The premium exit is NIQ or Circana buying the "who" layer they structurally lack. For them to pay a strategic premium, you must own something they can't just go get from the retailers directly. If the retailer owns the data and the relationship, NIQ bypasses you and goes to them — you're a modest tech tuck-in. If you own the cross-retailer visual aggregate and the rights, NIQ has to buy you to avoid being disrupted. Same cameras — the ownership structure is the entire difference between a tuck-in and a strategic premium.

  • GDPR / no cross-visit ID doesn't block this. The aggregate currency is cohort-level (k-anonymous category reads) — it needs no cross-visit ID and stays within GDPR when sensitive attributes are coarsened. The crown jewel is legally buildable in the EU.
  • Enablement isn't worthless — it funds you, de-risks you, and builds the network you aggregate. Just don't mistake the wedge for the prize.

Retailer-as-monetizer is the front door. The owned cross-retailer currency is the house. If the goal is valuation, build both — and never trade the aggregate-rights clause.

09 · Next steps — to accelerate

How to compress the timeline from where you stand

Position today: 8 chains engaged, 4 pilots live, 2 expansions committed (EuroOil 50, SuperZoo 10–15), 0 data rights signed, €0 brand revenue. Everything downstream is gated by the two stages sitting at zero. The strategy report's 30/90/180 plan still holds; these are the accelerators that break the zero fastest and plant the valuation asset early — in leverage order.

1
Sign SuperZoo data rights this month, riding the committed expansion this week → 30d

Warmest relationship, real POS, marketing advocate. Bundle the Data Partner Agreement into the 10–15 store expansion they already want (push 30) — with the aggregate-rights clause and the trading-data firewall in it. Frame it as win-win: they monetize their own audience, keep the majority, control the taps.

Accelerator: breaks the 0/8 rights zero, sets the reusable template, and plants the single valuation-critical clause in contract #1 — all in one move.
2
Turn the tobacco meeting into brand pull, then walk it into EuroOil next 2–3 weeks

Collect per-brand LOIs (250–500k CZK category pilots, "subject to retailer data agreement"). Then take "JTI/PMI are ready to pay X for a linked read on your stores" to EuroOil as the reason to sign POS + rights. Disclose the data probe to EuroOil before the meeting.

Accelerator: brand demand told to a retailer is the only thing that compresses a multi-year retailer cycle — it flips you from cold vendor to invited partner.
3
Productize the GymBeam category read so a brand study ships in days, not weeks 30–60d

The one-off insight study (150–500k, sellable the moment rights land) is your fastest revenue and your reference-logo engine. Templatize the GymBeam-style deck + pipeline so each new brand study is assembly, not a rebuild. Lead the pitch with measurement, not personas.

Accelerator: turns each rights signature into revenue in weeks, and every study into a case study that shortens the next retailer and brand conversation.
4
Ship one standard Data Partner Agreement template — firewall + aggregate license 30d

Every pipeline conversation should start from a signed-elsewhere template, not a blank page. Bake in the trading-data-vs-media-data firewall (defuses the objection on contact) and the perpetual aggregate license (the valuation asset). Reuse across SuperZoo → EuroOil → Teta → GymBeam.

Accelerator: a proven paper removes the slowest, most bespoke part of each deal — legal — and standardizes the one clause that matters for valuation.
5
Start the grocery-giant cycles now, in parallel — Billa, COOP, Albert start now, land 2028

They're CEO-level, ~2-year, 2028–29 outcomes — which is exactly why they start today. Open with "we'll build you a new 10M+ CZK profit center" (create revenue, don't invoice), backed by brand pull and the first case studies. COOP is already in your pipeline; treat it as a long-cycle seed, not a near-term line.

Accelerator: the only lever on a 2-year cycle is the start date. Every month you wait is a month later at the far end where the 100–200M cap lives.
6
Get the one missing number: retailer external-data spend this week

Ask the insider (or a retailer contact) what a Czech retailer actually spends per year on external data/analytics. Cheap, fast, and it resolves whether "operational intelligence sold to the retailer" is a real second revenue line or purely the barter for rights.

Accelerator: removes the biggest remaining unknown in the model before it costs a mispriced deal or a wrong slide in the raise.
The thread through all six

Steps 1, 4 and 5 all carry the aggregate-rights clause. That's deliberate: the fastest revenue comes from per-retailer deals, but the valuation comes from getting that one clause into the first two or three contracts — establishing the cross-retailer asset early, while retailers are still saying yes to expansion. Speed of revenue and size of valuation are two different races, run with the same signatures.

How this sharpens the report's sequencing (§7–§8): same order — SuperZoo → EuroOil (via the meeting) → Teta → giants — but every retailer approach now leads with the win-win framing, every brand pitch leads with measurement over personas, and every contract carries the aggregate-rights clause. The plan didn't change; the way you walk into each room did.
