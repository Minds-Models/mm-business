01 · The standout pattern

Fish & reptiles are a men's aisle — and facial hair makes it sharper

Dogs and cats — three-quarters of the store — are bought by everyone; no visual attribute meaningfully separates who buys them. The signal lives in the niche departments. There, one pattern is unmistakable and statistically solid: aquarium and reptile/terrarium baskets skew strongly male (sex association p < 0.001), and the skew intensifies with facial hair and greying hair. The clean-shaven index below average; stubbled and grey-haired shoppers index well above.

The picture it paints: the serious fishkeeper and reptile-keeper reads, on camera, as an older, bearded-or-stubbled man — and the clean-shaven or blonde shopper reads the opposite. Dogs and cats are universal; the exotic aisles have a face. That's a targeting hypothesis for what to show on a screen when the camera sees that profile.

02 · Which features "know" what you buy at all

Sex and self-presentation carry the department signal; most attributes don't

Ranking every attribute by how strongly it associates with which pet department is bought (chi-squared), a short list carries real signal — and it's dominated by sex / gender expression, plus a few grooming and age cues. Most of the 65 attributes (shoe colour, belt, watch…) show no department signal at all — a useful negative: the camera predicts pet type mainly through sex-linked and life-stage cues, not wardrobe detail.

03 · Basket value

For how much they spend, behaviour beats appearance

When the outcome is basket value rather than pet type, the appearance cues fade and behavioural ones lead — what the shopper is doing and carrying, not how they look:

Read: a shopper who is inspecting products (not just browsing) and isn't juggling a handbag ends up with a materially bigger basket — behaviour the camera can read live. The much-hyped "affluent look" barely registers; a highly-groomed shopper actually spends less.

04 · Read before using

What this is and isn't

  • Exploratory, not confirmatory. Sixty-five attributes × seven departments × value is a lot of comparisons; some associations will be chance. The ones highlighted here clear significance and sample thresholds and form a coherent story — but treat them as hypotheses to test, not settled facts.
  • Correlational and tangled. Male, stubble, grey hair and older age all travel together — this can't say which one "causes" the fish-aisle visit; they're facets of one shopper type.
  • Niche departments are small. Aquarium (4%) and reptile (3%) baskets mean the exciting lifts sit on tens of buyers. Directionally consistent, but not high-precision.
  • Dogs & cats are undifferentiated by design — the mass of the store is bought by everyone, so appearance can't target it. The value of visual signal is concentrated in the specialist aisles and in behaviour-for-basket-size.
  • One store, buyers only. Same scope limits as the rest of the pilot.

Method: checkout receipts (store CS314) matched to the buyer's visual profile; each basket assigned its dominant pet department by spend. Every usable attribute (≥400 tagged baskets, levels ≥150) tested against department (χ²) and against basket value (Kruskal–Wallis); level→department over-indexing reported where the department cell holds ≥25 buyers.
