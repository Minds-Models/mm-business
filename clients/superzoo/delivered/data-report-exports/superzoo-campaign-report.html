The July report ran on ten days. This one runs on forty-eight. That matters more than it sounds: several findings we reported in July were built on a few hundred receipts, and the larger pool has settled them — some confirmed, some not. Where a July number no longer holds, it is marked in this report rather than quietly dropped.

For the detailed product analysis below we use the window where receipt-level POS detail is available, 7 July – 4 August, joined receipt-by-receipt to the shopper at the till: 4,581 receipts matched on a key that produced zero collisions and 100% agreement on register ID. The before/after comparisons use two matched eight-day windows either side of the campaign start: 20–27 July against 28 July – 4 August.

02 · What the camera adds

The till records what sold. The camera records who bought it, and how they shopped.

Everything in this report comes from one of two places, and it is worth being explicit about which — because the sections that follow are only separable because of the second.

POS alone

  • What sold, when, at which till
  • Basket size, value and mix
  • Promotions redeemed
  • Buyers only

+ Visual layer

  • Shopper segment mix — with no loyalty card
  • Behaviour at the shelf: dwell, handling, engagement
  • Demographic signal by aisle
  • Non-buyers, and footfall as a denominator

The practical consequence runs through the whole report. Section 03 — the store fell 9.2% — is available from the register. Section 04 — who was in the store while it fell, and whether the 10.7% depth rise landed on the shoppers the campaign was aimed at — is not available from the register at any price. That distinction is the product.

Read: this is not a traffic problem. The same crowd arrived and put fewer things in the basket. Any explanation has to work at the basket level, not the door.

And the basket swung out of dogs

One department accounts for almost the whole decline. Dogs lost 70,000 Kč; cats, rodents and the specialist aisles picked up about half of it.

Why it matters for everything that follows: dog baskets are the big ones. A 7.6-point swing out of dogs is enough on its own to produce the fall in average basket, the fall in items per trip, and the sharp drop in dog-adjacent lines such as treats. Before crediting anything else with moving the numbers, this has to be subtracted first.

Decomposing the store's 10% basket decline into "different people came" versus "the same people spent less" gives a clear answer: the mix explains 2% of it. The other 98% is the same personas spending less. This is a behavioural period, not a compositional one.

On 2,721 shoppers with a decisive p-value, this is the best-powered finding in the pilot — and it is unavailable from the register by construction, because the till cannot see what happened before the scan. It also points at a lever that needs no media spend: engagement is something staffing and shelf design influence directly. Alongside it, the visual layer separates departments where spend alone cannot: men index 1.75× on the fish and reptile aisles (9.3% of their baskets against 5.3% for women), and facial hair carries a 1.47× lift into reptile specifically. Those demographic cells rest on tens of receipts and are directional; the engagement result is not.

The practical read: "pet in store" effectively means "small dog." Anything built for these shoppers — a greeting, a fitting area, a screen prompt, a treat station — should be sized and stocked for small dogs first. What to do with it: this is a non-food trip and it announces itself at the door. A shopper with their dog is more than five times as likely to buy walking gear and two and a half times as likely to buy a toy, their basket runs 553 Kč against 390, and food drops from three-fifths of the basket to under half. Accessories, fitting and treats are what the visit is for — the clearest case in the pilot for putting the right thing in front of the right shopper, because the cue is unmistakable and visible before they reach a shelf.

The pattern across the eight-week pool. Persona basket values sit in a narrow band — two independent cuts put it at 399–457 and 416–458 Kč — while the department each persona shops diverges sharply (p < 0.001). Personas are strongly predictive of what a shopper came for. They are not predictive of how much that shopper spends. That is the split which decides how the segments are used: for range, placement and content, not for forecasting spend.

Two things shape how to read this. The period itself was falling — the dog department lost 21.3% while cats grew 22.3% — so the dog-side creatives were pushing against their own aisle and the cat-side ones were swimming with theirs. And the one metric that rose everywhere, units per line, is arithmetic rather than persuasion: lines fell 26% while units fell 19%, so the average line deepened because the shallow lines disappeared.

Half the campaign was aimed at products the store barely sells. Leashes move three to four units a week here, and cat treats to students covered one to two lines in each window. Whatever those creatives did to shoppers, the volumes are too small for a result to exist either way — which is the first thing to fix in the next brief.

06 · What this buys

The levers this opens, and where each lands in the P&L

Sizing the next test. Day-to-day variation in treat depth is 0.337 units per line on a mean of 1.93. Detecting an 8% effect at conventional power needs roughly 74 store-days per arm — ten stores for a week, or five stores for a fortnight. This campaign ran on eight store-days, so the next one should be scoped against that number rather than against a calendar.

Five levers, ordered by how well each is evidenced today

Screen targeting is one of five, and on this evidence not the nearest one. The others do not depend on the campaign result at all:

Vision-only capabilityAbsent from POS becauseP&L lever
Engagement at the shelfthe till sees no intent, only outcomesStaffing & assisted sell → conversion, labour ROI. +21% basket, p<0.0001 — the best-evidenced lever here.
Footfall vs purchasenon-buyers leave no receiptConverting traffic you already pay for → revenue with no media spend

Demographic signal by aislethe till cannot say who a category servesPlacement & local assortment → revenue

Note the ordering. The lever with the strongest evidence behind it — engagement, on 2,721 shoppers at p<0.0001 — costs nothing in media and is executed by store operations, not marketing. The lever this campaign tested sits last, because it is the one we cannot yet evidence. On present data that is the honest sequence to work through.

Three design rules follow directly, and they are what we would apply to the next campaign:

  • Promote what the store sells in volume. Half of this campaign was aimed at products moving single-digit units a week. Penetration determines whether a result can exist at all, before creative quality matters at all.
  • Name the metric before switching on, and vary exposure deliberately. Constant exposure produces a before/after that seasonality can always explain — as it does here.
  • Measure against the store, not against last week. Every number in section 04 is quoted against the store's own movement. Without that baseline a −21% result reads as −21%; with it, the honest figure is −12 points of relative underperformance, and a +11% depth rise reads as what it is.
