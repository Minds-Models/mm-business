01 · Linkage

Receipts matched to checkout visits by time correlation

The POS feed has no customer identity — receipt numbers are transaction ids, not loyalty ids — so we matched each receipt to the person standing at the register when it closed. The camera and POS clocks turned out to be already in sync: presence peaks exactly at the receipt's closing timestamp and collapses within seconds after it — people pay and walk away.

Matching rule: candidates are non-staff visitors present at the payment moment (recognized staff are excluded, as is anyone parked at the desk longer than 20 minutes). Among candidates, the buyer is the one whose departure aligns with the receipt closing; each person can claim one receipt. Median gap between matched visitor's exit and receipt close: 9.8 s. Every match carries a confidence score (share of the winning candidate's weight; single-candidate matches ≈ 1.0), so every result below can be re-checked on the strict ≥0.5 subset.

02 · Personas × money

Personas that look different, spend different: the top persona out-spends the store by 25–55%

Suburban Men in their 40s are the store's most valuable persona per visit — 533 Kč average basket against the 426 Kč store average (p = 0.008 vs the rest), rising to 661 Kč on high-confidence matches. Suburban Women in their 30s are the highest-traffic persona (highest receipt count) but run ~12% below the store average (p = 0.03). The ordering is stable across all-matched, confidence-weighted, and strict subsets.

The gradient is largely monotone and mirror-imaged: young personas skew to house brands, older personas to VAFO's premium supplier brands (persona × brand-group composition χ² p = 0.007; receipt-level spread directional at this n). The raw age feature tells the same story independently — private-label share falls from 40% (teens) to 23% (60s+). And the branded-food buyers are the biggest tickets: baskets dominated by VAFO brands average 599 Kč vs 501 Kč private-label-dominated and 428 Kč third-party.

Persona-level brand leads worth watching (n = 10–30 receipts, directional): Budget Conscious → Kočkolit economy litter 2.0×; Young Women 20s → Dog Fantasy accessories 1.9×; Suburban Men 40s → Brit 1.2×. Cross-sell structure is also visible — treats attach to everything (treats × dry food in 97 receipts, treats × wet in 91, toys × treats in 64): "add a treat" is the store's universal attach play.

Why this matters commercially. Private label is where the retailer's margin lives; VAFO is the key supplier relationship. This table is both a margin lever (the high-ticket, low-PL personas — Men 40s, Retirees — are the PL conversion target; premium house lines like Elbeville are the vehicle) and a retail-media asset (provable "who buys Brit" audiences to sell campaigns against).

Caveat: brand matching is a curated lexicon over Czech product names + supplier-prefix extension; ~21% of revenue stays unclassified (live animals, generic goods). Production needs the client's item master — a one-file ask.

05 · Single features × spend

Individual features carry some signal — but personas (feature bundles) tell the richer story

  • Sex: male-presenting buyers average 472 Kč vs 405 Kč female (p = 0.006) — consistent with the persona ranking, visible even in this single raw feature.
  • Life stage & age: weak on basket value, strong on basket composition (the dog/cat splits above). Value and composition are different products: one prices a visit, the other picks the content shown.
  • Grooming state: "high" grooming → 264 Kč vs 441 Kč standard (p = 0.04, n = 39) — curious, small sample, watch it.
  • Logo prominence: shoppers wearing discreet/no-logo clothing spend 35% of their basket on private label vs 28% for prominent-logo wearers (p = 0.13, directional only — small effect, watch with more data).

Honest null: apparent_spending_tier — the model's visual guess at affluence — does not predict pet-shop basket size (budget 413 Kč vs mid-range 438 Kč, n.s.; only 13 "premium" sightings). Dressing well doesn't buy more dog food. This is a useful finding: it says which registry attributes deserve weight in commercial models and which don't — something only a POS pairing can tell us.

06 · Read this before quoting numbers

Scope & caveats

  • One store, 10 usable days. POS covers 16 Jun–2 Jul, but the cashdesk camera went live 23 Jun — the first week of POS (~1,000 receipts) has no camera counterpart. The 6 Jul export file is empty and 3–5 Jul is missing; a continuing daily feed keeps this analysis alive.
  • Linkage is probabilistic. 96% of receipts get a match, but only 109 are single-candidate; median confidence 0.41. All headline claims were re-checked on the strict ≥0.5 subset and survive directionally; treat cell-level lifts (n≈10–30) as leads, not conclusions.
  • Buyer-conditioned by construction. Cashdesk pairing only sees people who bought. Conversion/propensity questions need the sales-floor camera as the denominator — it is already recording.
  • Feature coverage is ~60% of cashdesk visitors (extraction eligibility); matched receipts are biased toward longer, camera-facing visits.
  • Persona overall spread: Kruskal–Wallis across all 8 personas p = 0.14 — the omnibus test is not yet significant at this sample size; the two flagged personas are individually significant. Ten more weeks of feed settles it.
  • Privacy: analysis done on de-identified aggregates; every reported cell has n ≥ 10 receipts (brand cells ≥ 11); sensitive attributes used only in aggregate, per our data-governance policy.

Method: receipts from 18 daily POS exports (store CS314) matched to anonymous checkout visits from the cashdesk camera by time correlation — a shopper is credited with a receipt when their departure lines up with the moment the receipt closes; staff are excluded and every match carries a confidence score used to re-check all findings on a strict subset. No identities are stored; all reported cells aggregate 10+ receipts. Tests: Mann-Whitney / Kruskal–Wallis on basket totals. All figures CZK. Analysis 12 Jul 2026.
